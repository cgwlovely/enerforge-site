The largest mine-electrification loads may be invisible in today’s electricity data

Sector study 6 August 2026 12-minute read Market sizing · Mine energy · Australia

← All case studies Discuss a question

Australia’s largest future mining electricity loads may not appear in today’s electricity data. The mining sector consumed 299.6 petajoules of diesel in 2023–24, and a reconciliation of the national fuel and generation accounts indicates that most of it is used in mobile equipment rather than power stations. Converted to an electrical equivalent, that fuel is a theoretical average load of 3.9–4.65 GW, approximately 70–90 per cent of the sector’s current electricity consumption. This is an upper bound, not a forecast of electrification: it shows why screening mines by existing electrical load can miss the sites with the largest potential future demand.

Question
How much additional electrical load could mining-fleet electrification create, and which sites would be missed by a screen based on current electricity demand?
Basis
Australian Energy Statistics 2023–24: national diesel consumption and oil-fired generation accounts and current mining electricity consumption; the NGER and National Pollutant Inventory registers; state generation licences; Heliovulcan’s site-level screen of 370 Australian mines.
Method
The national diesel account is first reconciled against reported oil-fired generation and identified mine and remote-town power stations. Reported mining diesel consumption is then converted to an electrical equivalent using a range for diesel-engine and electric-drivetrain efficiency, and compared with the sector’s current electricity demand.
Finding
Most Australian diesel is used in vehicles and mobile equipment. Full conversion of reported mining diesel implies a theoretical average electrical load of 3.9–4.65 GW, equivalent to approximately 70–90 per cent of mining’s current electricity consumption. Some of the largest potential loads may sit at sites that report little or no electricity use today.
Limits
The national accounts do not identify how much mining diesel is used by haul trucks rather than other mobile or stationary equipment. The estimate does not model fleet suitability, adoption, charging peaks, mine life, grid capacity, generation requirements, capital cost or replacement timing. It is an upper-bound energy conversion, not an addressable-market forecast.
At a glance
  1. Only about 4 per cent of Australia’s 1,269.9 PJ of diesel is burned to make electricity: a ceiling of roughly 551 MW of average output, corroborated from the generation side of the accounts.
  2. Mining’s 299.6 PJ is almost entirely mobile plant. Most mine power-station diesel is booked under electricity supply, where about 390 MW of contractor-operated generation sits; two reconciliations land within one per cent of each other.
  3. Converted to electricity, that diesel is a theoretical 3.9–4.65 GW of average load, about 70–90 per cent of mining’s current 5,339 MW of electricity consumption. It is an energy upper bound, not charging capacity and not the addressable market.
  4. The largest potential loads may sit at sites that report little or no electricity today, so a screen keyed on current electrical load misses them; fuel tax credits (about A$3.9 billion a year on the whole pool) and fleet-replacement timing decide where demand appears first.

A site with little reported electricity can still become a large electrical load

Market screens for mine energy work usually start from what a site consumes today: purchased electricity, or the capacity of the power station on site. A diesel-powered mine records most of its energy as fuel, not electricity. Its haul trucks, dozers, drills and light vehicles appear in the national accounts as mining diesel; they appear in the electricity data hardly at all. A screen keyed on current electrical load therefore ranks such a site as small, or as no target, when its future load could be among the largest in the sector.

One screened site shows the effect. A remote gold operation registers zero generation and zero purchased electricity because it runs on diesel plant. Converting its energy use to an electrical equivalent gives an 8.7–10.4 MW average load, implying an installed station of 25–35 MW, several times the size of a real power station at a neighbouring operation in the same group. A layer built on who has a power station today classifies that site as nothing at all.

The largest new load will appear at sites that consume little or no electricity today, which is precisely where a screen keyed on existing generation never looks.

Most of the diesel is on wheels

The claim that mining diesel is mostly mobile load has to be shown, not assumed. National energy statistics publish consumption by industry division and by fuel, and generation by fuel, from the same statistical spine; reconciling the diesel account against the generation account, for 2023–24, shows how little of the fuel is burned in power stations.

Of 1,269.9 PJ of national diesel consumption, roughly 50 PJ is burned to generate electricity, a ceiling of about 551 MW of average output that includes mine stations, remote-town stations and factory standby sets. The generation side of the accounts corroborates it: 4,823 GWh of oil-fired generation, or 551 MW. Subtract the 148 registered remote-town power stations (45.4 MW, all state-utility town supply) and roughly 37.8 PJ under electricity supply is unexplained, equivalent to about 390 MW. Approached from the generation side in the three states where off-grid operation is material, the same figure appears: 3,779 GWh, less those states’ town stations, leaves 387 MW.

A consumption-side reconciliation and a generation-side reconciliation therefore produce estimates within approximately one per cent. They use different national totals but share the identified remote-town fleet as a subtraction. The explanation is structural: at most Australian mine sites the power station is owned and operated by an independent power producer under a long-term contract, and its fuel is reported under the operator’s industry classification, electricity supply, not the mine’s. That attribution is an inference drawn from the accounts, not a field in any register. Its corollary is the point that matters here: if most mine power-station diesel is booked elsewhere, then mining’s own 299.6 PJ is almost entirely mobile plant.

Most remaining diesel use is mobile or dispersed rather than centralised electricity generation: road transport 679 PJ, agriculture 89 PJ, rail 52 PJ, construction 27 PJ; smaller stationary and standby uses are spread across commercial services and manufacturing, whose energy is mainly purchased electricity.

Full conversion of reported mining diesel implies 3.9–4.65 GW of theoretical average load

Converting the 299.6 PJ to an electrical equivalent, with diesel engines at 35–45 per cent end-use efficiency and electric drivetrains at 85–92 per cent, gives a theoretical average electrical load of 3.9–4.65 GW. Australian mining consumed 5,339 MW of electricity in 2023–24, so full conversion would add approximately 70–90 per cent to the sector’s current electricity demand. In Western Australian metal mining, where 120.3 PJ of the diesel is burned, it would multiply off-grid station capacity several times over.

The table is an energy conversion of the reported fuel, by segment. It does not say that every petajoule is a haul truck: the national accounts do not split mining diesel between haul fleets and other mobile or stationary equipment, and the figure should be read as the whole reported pool.

Average energy is not charging capacity

The estimate converts annual energy use into an average electrical equivalent. Actual charging capacity could be higher, because vehicles charge during limited operating windows and because the power system must accommodate coincident demand, losses and operational reserve. The figure is therefore neither a generation requirement, nor a connection capacity, nor a battery size.

The theoretical load is not the addressable market

The 3.9–4.65 GW is an upper bound on energy. The commercially addressable load sits at the bottom of a funnel whose lower layers this study does not quantify.

Fuel tax credits reduce the value of each litre displaced

Off-road diesel in Australian mining attracts a full fuel tax credit, so an electric fleet competes against a subsidised price, and every litre displaced gives the credit back. At the full off-road rate, the reported mining diesel pool is associated with approximately A$3.9 billion a year of fuel tax credits (metal ore and quarrying about A$1.9 billion, coal mining about A$2.0 billion). This is a national-order exposure, not the cost of an achievable electrification programme: only fuel actually displaced would forgo the credit. Any mine energy business case has to be net of it.

The first projects will be selected by timing, not diesel volume alone

Haul-truck service life and remaining mine life are often of the same order, and a mine whose reserve life will not outlast one more fleet cycle will not buy the fleet. The window, where there is one, is an alignment. A site is a candidate when it meets all of the following:

  • large mobile diesel exposure;
  • fleet replacement approaching;
  • sufficient remaining mine life to amortise the fleet and the power system;
  • grid or onsite generation that can be expanded;
  • a charging duty cycle that the operation can accommodate;
  • a power contract that can be adjusted before its term;
  • owner and operator interests that align on who funds the upgrade.

Fleet age is not publicly available site by site. Mine life and contract expiry are. That asymmetry should decide the order in which a screen asks its questions.

Implications for market screening

  1. Do not rank mines only by current electricity consumption.
  2. Estimate mobile diesel exposure separately from stationary generation.
  3. Treat the electrical-equivalent load as an upper bound.
  4. Check fleet replacement and mine life before modelling infrastructure.
  5. Model fuel tax credits and charging capacity explicitly.
  6. Identify who owns the fleet and who must fund the power-system upgrade.

The national diesel total is not an electrification forecast. It is the starting account. That account shows why current electricity demand is an incomplete guide to future mining load: a diesel-powered site can appear small in electricity data and become a material power project when its mobile equipment is electrified. Across the sector, reported mining diesel represents a theoretical 3.9–4.65 GW of average electrical demand. The commercial market will be smaller. Its size depends on which equipment can be electrified, when fleets are replaced, whether mine life supports the investment, how charging changes peak demand, whether power supply can expand and who bears the loss of fuel tax credits. Those questions have to be resolved facility by facility.

How this was done

A bottom-up site list, tested against a national account built a different way

Heliovulcan maintains a site-level screen of Australian mine on-site generation — 370 sites, with fuel, scale band and grid position established from regulatory filings rather than assumed from industry codes. That screen answers “which sites” but cannot answer “is that all of them”, because a list has no denominator.

The denominator comes from national energy statistics, which publish consumption by industry division and by fuel, and generation by fuel, from the same statistical spine. Building the market from the sites and then testing it against those totals gives two things a site list alone never gives: a ceiling, and a residual that has to be named.

One check governs whether any of this counts. Two figures agreeing is only evidence if their inputs do not overlap — a facility’s reported emissions and its regulator-published intensity factor will always agree, because one is built from the other. The 390 MW finding in this note is reported because two calculations reached it from inputs with no shared source: an industry-classified energy balance on one side, contractor project registers and state generation licences on the other.

Sources Australian energy statistics 2023–24 (consumption by industry and fuel; generation by fuel and state); national greenhouse and energy reporting register of designated generation facilities; Safeguard Mechanism emissions-intensity determinations; national pollutant inventory; state generation licences and environmental approvals. All publicly published.
Period Financial year 2023–24 throughout, except the generation reconciliation, which uses 2022–23 where 2023–24 was not available. The mixing is deliberate and is flagged where it occurs.
Assumptions Conversion from fuel to electrical output assumes 33 per cent thermal efficiency. Across a 30–38 per cent range the 390 MW figure moves between roughly 340 and 450 MW, without changing the conclusion. Limitations Some state and sub-division cells are suppressed in the official tables for confidentiality, so sub-totals do not always sum to division totals; division totals are the reliable figures.
