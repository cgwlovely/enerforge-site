Lowering the Safeguard threshold would reduce the baseline floor for facilities already covered

Quantitative analysis 7 August 2026 13-minute read Climate policy · Industrial assets · Australia

← All research Discuss a question

Debate about a lower Safeguard threshold has focused on the facilities that would enter the scheme. The threshold also sets the minimum baseline for facilities already covered.

Lowering the threshold from 100,000 to 75,000 tonnes would reduce their aggregate FY2029–30 baseline by at least 0.92 Mt CO2-e. The consultation paper quantifies the 4.1 Mt of emissions that would enter coverage, but not this separate effect on facilities already covered.

Question
How would a lower coverage threshold affect facilities already inside the Safeguard Mechanism?
Basis
The FY2024–25 and FY2023–24 Safeguard registers; the Safeguard Rule ss 10(1), 11, 56(4) and 58B; the consultation paper’s table 3.3; Heliovulcan’s dataset covering 88 gold operations.
Method
The analysis projects each covered facility’s published baseline to FY2029–30 using the legislated emissions reduction contribution. It then applies minimum baselines of 100,000, 75,000, 50,000 and 25,000 tonnes and compares the resulting aggregate baselines. Coverage status is taken from the register’s section 58B disclosure, and national totals are reconciled with the department’s published estimates.
Finding
Twenty-four covered facilities currently sit on the 100,000-tonne minimum baseline; 45 are projected to reach it by FY2029–30. Reducing the threshold to 75,000 tonnes would lower the aggregate baseline for existing covered facilities by at least 0.92 Mt CO2-e a year. This is separate from the 4.1 Mt of emissions associated with newly covered facilities.
Limits
The model holds facility production and emissions intensity constant apart from the legislated decline factor. Public data do not provide emissions for individual sub-threshold facilities. For facilities already on the minimum baseline, the register does not disclose the lower calculated value behind it, so the estimated baseline reduction is a lower bound.
At a glance
  1. Twenty-four covered facilities currently sit on the minimum baseline; 45 are projected to reach it by FY2029–30.
  2. At a 75,000-tonne threshold, existing covered facilities would lose at least 0.92 Mt CO2-e of aggregate baseline a year.
  3. The effect falls disproportionately on resources facilities: 32 of the 45 projected floor-bound facilities are in metal ore mining, coal mining or oil and gas.

The threshold changes two parts of the scheme

Three current policy processes address related parts of the Safeguard Mechanism.

  • The Carbon Leakage Review considers border adjustment and trade-exposed baselines.
  • The Climate Change Authority considers the post-2030 decline rate and onsite abatement.
  • The department’s consultation paper considers the coverage threshold and other scheme settings.

This analysis addresses one interaction in the department’s paper: the relationship between the coverage threshold and the statutory minimum baseline. The paper’s threshold table gives the new facilities that a lower threshold would bring into the scheme: 75,000 tonnes admits 48 facilities carrying 4.1 Mt of emissions, 50,000 tonnes admits 108 facilities and 7.8 Mt, and 25,000 tonnes admits 291 facilities and 14.2 Mt, with the largest increases in metal ore mining and oil and gas extraction. Section 3.5.1 states that the minimum baseline would fall with the coverage threshold:

“Under either approach, the minimum baseline — currently 100,000 tonnes — would fall to be consistent with the new coverage threshold.”

Safeguard Mechanism Review Consultation Paper, August 2026, section 3.5.1

The consultation paper quantifies the emissions and facilities that would enter the scheme, but it does not quantify the resulting reduction in baselines for facilities already covered. The reconstruction below indicates that the effect is material.

How the minimum baseline works

The Safeguard Rule uses different baseline values for compliance obligations and credit issuance. Section 10(1) prevents a covered facility’s compliance baseline from falling below 100,000 tonnes. Section 56(4), however, measures credit issuance against the lower baseline that would have applied without that floor.

Compliance obligation. A facility’s baseline is set to 100,000 tonnes if the number calculated under the ordinary rules comes out below 100,000.

National Greenhouse and Energy Reporting (Safeguard Mechanism) Rule 2015, s10(1)

Credit issuance. Credits are issued against the baseline that would have been calculated “if subsection 10(1) had not been enacted” — that is, against the unfloored number.

Same instrument, s56(4)

The two provisions serve different purposes. The minimum baseline limits the compliance burden on smaller or declining facilities. Using the unfloored baseline for credit issuance prevents the same facilities from receiving credits solely because their compliance baseline has been held at the minimum.

For a facility that remains covered while sitting on the minimum baseline, reported emissions will generally exceed its compliance baseline: the facility is covered because its emissions exceed 100,000 tonnes, and its baseline, once floored, is 100,000 tonnes. In FY2024–25, all 24 covered facilities on the 100,000-tonne minimum reported emissions above that level. The lowest reported 101,144 tonnes and the median 127,011 tonnes.

The other 19 facilities shown at 100,000 tonnes were no longer designated large facilities for the year and therefore carried no Safeguard obligation. They are excluded from the estimated effect of changing the minimum baseline; the register evidence for them, including one credited against its unfloored baseline under section 56(4), is in the technical appendix.

Falling below the threshold does not always end coverage: a facility inside a multi-year monitoring period stays in, and one facility in the 2024–25 table is covered on emissions of 80,908 tonnes on that basis. It is the only such case in the table.

The register shows the covered group on the minimum baseline growing year by year.

A covered facility on the minimum baseline has emissions above 100,000 tonnes and a baseline of exactly 100,000 tonnes. It cannot comply without credits unless it falls below the coverage threshold.

The floor cohort is concentrated among smaller-emitting facilities in resource sectors. Of the 45 covered facilities projected to reach the floor by 2029–30, 17 are metal ore mines, 10 are coal mines and 5 are oil and gas facilities. Metal ore mining accounts for 38 per cent of the projected floor cohort, compared with 24 per cent of the covered register; coal mining runs the other way, at 22 per cent of the cohort against 32 per cent of the register, because coal mines are on average larger emitters and stay above the floor.

The effect on existing facilities

Because the minimum baseline moves with the coverage threshold, the threshold decision also determines whether the baselines of the 45 floor-bound facilities resume declining.

Moving the threshold from 100,000 to 75,000 tonnes admits 48 facilities carrying 4.1 Mt of emissions. Those emissions are not an obligation: a newly covered facility receives a baseline, and only the gap between its emissions and that baseline must be covered. Under the department’s Option 2, where new entrants start near the top of the decline curve, the first-year obligation is close to zero.

The 0.92 Mt figure measures a different effect: baseline that stops being granted to facilities already regulated, reporting and covered. That reduction translates directly into a tighter aggregate compliance limit for existing facilities. It applies through existing reporting and compliance arrangements, but it is not included in the consultation paper’s threshold table.

Why the effect is nonlinear

Three qualifications affect the estimate.

The estimate is a lower bound

For the 24 covered facilities already on the minimum baseline, the published 100,000 tonnes is the floored value; the calculated baseline behind it is lower, by an amount the register does not disclose. Projecting 100,000 forward therefore overstates their unfloored FY2029–30 baseline and understates the difference between the minimum baseline and the calculated baseline.

The effect does not change evenly with the threshold

The effect changes unevenly because 24 facilities share the same published 100,000-tonne baseline and therefore project to the same 72,838 tonnes in 2029–30. At a 75,000-tonne threshold they remain just under the new minimum and stay floored; at 50,000 tonnes they move entirely off the floor. The precise transition depends on calculated baseline values that are not publicly disclosed. The distribution of projected baselines is charted in the appendix.

The analysis does not identify a preferred threshold

This analysis does not assess which coverage threshold should be adopted. It identifies an effect that should be included when the options are compared. The department’s reasoning against a 25,000-tonne threshold (291 new facilities, 18 new industry groups, administrative cost outrunning abatement) is not examined here.

Which sectors are most exposed

The practical effect is concentrated in resource sectors.

Both the department and the Authority raise the same concern. Net emissions fell 7.4 per cent and then 5.5 per cent across the two post-reform years, while gross emissions, before credits, fell 1.9 and 2.4 per cent. The difference was met with credits. The Authority states that material onsite abatement “appears limited to a few facilities”.

The facility register shows that credit use is concentrated in resource sectors.

Four-fifths of offset-credit surrender comes from three resource sectors: coal mining 48 per cent, oil and gas extraction 18 per cent and metal ore mining 15 per cent of the 10.5 million credits surrendered in FY2024–25. All non-resource sectors combined account for 19 per cent. The sector chart and totals, in the appendix →

Of the 24 facilities that surrendered offset credits equal to 30 per cent or more of their baseline — the threshold at which the regulator requires a written explanation of why more was not done on site — 22 are coal or metal ore mines: 12 and 10 respectively.

The measures under consideration, including quantitative limits on credit use, discounting so that more than one credit is needed per tonne, and vintage restrictions, would apply across the scheme. Their immediate effect would fall disproportionately on mining facilities. All three assume that the party holding the baseline can respond by investing on site. For a large and identifiable class of mines, it cannot.

A split incentive at contractor-operated mine power stations

At an off-grid mine, onsite abatement means changing what the power station burns or adding solar and storage in front of it. That is the only lever of any scale, and at most sites the mine does not hold it.

We have gone through the Australian gold sector site by site — 88 operations carrying a Safeguard or pollutant-inventory identity, with station ownership, storage and contract expiry established from contractor project registers, state generation licences and company filings.

Station ownership was examined for 39 gold operations with onsite generation. Thirty-five stations were contractor-owned, typically by one of about seven fund-owned independent power producers under a build-own-operate contract with a term of a decade or more; two were miner-owned and two remained unresolved.

  • Storage sits inside the contractor’s asset. Fifteen of the operations disclosed battery storage; in each identified case, the battery was installed within the power provider’s asset and contract structure.
  • The obligation and the asset sit with different parties. The mine reports the generation emissions and holds the baseline. The contractor may own the plant, choose the fuel and receive the fuel saving.
  • Contract expiry is rarely public. A contract expiry date was identified in public sources for nine of the 88 operations. No public expiry date was found for the remainder.

Source: Heliovulcan site-by-site build over 88 Australian gold operations carrying a Safeguard or pollutant-inventory identity, from contractor project registers, state generation licences and company filings. None of the three facts — station ownership, storage, contract expiry — appears in any national register. Percentages exclude 22 sites recorded as not yet checked; a blank storage field means no disclosure was found, never that no battery exists.

This creates a split incentive. The mine holds the Safeguard obligation, while the contractor may own the generating asset and control investment in fuel switching, solar or storage. A higher offset price raises the cost on the miner and leaves the investment decision where it already sits, with a counterparty whose contract does not price carbon and typically has years left to run. Before strengthening the price signal, the review should consider whether the party carrying the obligation also controls the relevant abatement asset; for this class of facility that is knowable only site by site.

The same caution applies, for a different reason, to coal: tightening coal baselines creates a compliance cost at mines that are all grid-connected, and no generation-side market. The reasoning is in the appendix →

The department projects the largest increase in facility numbers under a lower threshold in metal ore mining and oil and gas extraction. Heliovulcan’s mine screen indicates that many potentially relevant mining facilities are remote, off-grid or weak-grid operations with onsite power systems; the population figures are in the appendix. The gold-sector sample indicates that third-party power-station ownership may be common, but ownership would need to be verified facility by facility and across other commodities. For project developers and policymakers, the relevant unit is therefore not only the covered facility but also the ownership and contract structure of its power supply.

Implications for the threshold review

  1. Include the baseline-floor effect in the options table. The consultation currently quantifies newly covered emissions but not the reduction in baselines for existing facilities.
  2. Separate coverage from compliance impact. Emissions entering the scheme are not the same as the immediate surrender obligation created by the change.
  3. Test whether the regulated party controls the abatement asset. At contractor-operated mine power stations, the facility carrying the Safeguard obligation may not control investment in generation, fuel switching or storage.

A threshold decision should therefore be assessed against both populations: facilities entering the scheme and facilities already covered whose minimum baseline would fall.
