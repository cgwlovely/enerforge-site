Lowering the Safeguard threshold also tightens baselines for facilities already covered

Quantitative analysis 7 August 2026 15-minute read Climate policy · Industrial assets · Australia

← All research Discuss a question

Three papers now sit open on Australia’s industrial carbon settings: the department’s Safeguard Mechanism review consultation paper, released 7 August; the Climate Change Authority’s annual progress advice, which consulted through to 9 August; and the final report of the Carbon Leakage Review. Between them they ask a dozen questions. One of the quieter ones — whether to lower the 100,000-tonne coverage threshold — is being discussed as a question about who comes into the scheme. Rebuild the register and it turns out to be a bigger question about who is already in it.

Question
What changes when the Safeguard coverage threshold is lowered?
Basis
The FY2024–25 and FY2023–24 Safeguard registers; the Safeguard Rule ss 10(1), 11, 56(4) and 57; the consultation paper’s table 3.3; Heliovulcan’s site-by-site build of 88 gold operations.
Method
Each covered facility’s baseline is rescaled from its filed emissions reduction contribution to 0.657 and tested against the statutory minimum at four candidate thresholds; covered facilities are identified from the register’s s 58B note; totals are reconciled to the department’s published figures.
Finding
Twenty-four covered facilities sit on the 100,000 t floor today and 45 do by 2029–30. A 75,000 t threshold withdraws about 0.92 Mt a year of baseline from facilities already covered, against 4.1 Mt of newly covered emissions, and that effect is absent from the consultation table.
Limits
Production and intensity are not re-derived for every facility; sub-threshold facility emissions cannot be computed from public data; for the 24 already floored, the filed value is the floored one, so the held-back tonnage is a lower bound.
At a glance
  1. The scheme has a statutory floor. A baseline calculated below 100,000 tonnes is set to 100,000 tonnes. In the 2024–25 published table, 43 facilities sat at exactly that number — but only 24 of them are still covered; the other 19 have dropped out of the scheme and stayed on the list. Project the covered register forward on the legislated decline schedule and by 2029–30 it is 45 of 208.
  2. The floor is worth at least 1.0 Mt a year by 2029–30 — baseline held above where the 4.9-point schedule would otherwise put it, at facilities that still carry an obligation. The department projects aggregate baselines at 96 Mt against a 100 Mt target; on this reconstruction, about a quarter of that margin is the floor.
  3. Lowering the coverage threshold lowers the floor with it. The consultation paper says so in one sentence and does not cost it. At a 75,000-tonne threshold the held-back baseline falls from 0.99 Mt to 0.07 Mt — a tightening of 0.92 Mt on facilities already covered, against the 4.1 Mt of newly covered emissions the paper does put in a table.
  4. Seventy-one per cent of the floor-bound cohort is resources — 17 metal ore mines, 10 coal mines, 5 oil and gas facilities. The floor is, disproportionately, a mining instrument.
  5. The on-site abatement problem both papers are worried about is also a mining problem. Coal, metal ore and oil and gas account for 81 per cent of offset credits surrendered for compliance — 79 per cent once deemed surrenders are added — and 22 of the 24 facilities that used credits for 30 per cent or more of their baseline are mines.
  6. For one large slice of it, the incentive does not reach the owner. Of the gold operations we have verified that have their own power station, 90 per cent do not own it — it belongs to a contractor on a long build-own-operate contract. Every disclosed battery at those sites is on the contractor’s asset.

The threshold proposal changes two different boundaries

The three documents are one review seen from three angles. The Carbon Leakage Review recommended a border adjustment for cement first and the removal of concessional trade-exposed baselines once a border adjustment covers a commodity; the Climate Change Authority was asked about the post-2030 decline rate and on-site abatement, and puts the rate at roughly 4.5 to 7 per cent a year against an indicative 3.285; the department’s consultation paper carries everything else, including the coverage threshold, and states that the review “is not intended to make fundamental reforms”. The three documents, in the appendix →

Then, forty lines later, in a passage about how baselines would be set for newly covered facilities, comes this:

“Under either approach, the minimum baseline — currently 100,000 tonnes — would fall to be consistent with the new coverage threshold.”

Safeguard Mechanism Review Consultation Paper, August 2026, section 3.5.1

That sentence is not costed anywhere in the paper. The consultation paper does not quantify this effect, although the reconstruction indicates that it is material.

The floor

Two provisions of the Safeguard Rules work against each other, and the interaction is deliberate, and it has consequences the review does not appear to have priced.

The obligation has a floor. A facility’s baseline is set to 100,000 tonnes if the number calculated under the ordinary rules comes out below 100,000.

National Greenhouse and Energy Reporting (Safeguard Mechanism) Rule 2015, s10(1)

The reward does not. Credits are issued against the baseline that would have been calculated “if subsection 10(1) had not been enacted” — that is, against the unfloored number.

Same instrument, s56(4)

The logic is sound. Without the floor, a shrinking facility’s baseline would chase its emissions down to nothing and the administrative cost of covering it would exceed the abatement. Without the asymmetry, a facility parked on the floor could bank credits for abatement it was already going to make. So the floor caps the obligation and the asymmetry cancels the reward.

What that produces is a facility with no headroom. For a covered facility the floored baseline is numerically the coverage threshold: the facility is covered because its emissions exceed 100,000 tonnes, and its baseline, once floored, is 100,000 tonnes. A facility on the floor therefore has no headroom at all — every year it is covered is a year above its baseline. The 2024–25 register bears this out without a single exception: all 24 covered facilities on the floor emitted more than 100,000 tonnes, the lowest at 101,144 and the median at 127,780.

Nineteen facilities in the 2024–25 table emit below their floored baseline and carry no obligation because they are no longer designated large facilities; one of them was issued credits against its unfloored baseline, which is section 56(4) working as drafted. The register evidence, in the appendix →

Falling below the threshold does not always end coverage: a facility inside a multi-year monitoring period stays in, and one facility in the 2024–25 table is covered on emissions of 80,908 tonnes on that basis. It is the exception.

The register shows how fast the covered group on the floor is growing.

The two panels share a time axis but not a scale — a count and a tonnage are different measures and never belong on one pair of axes. The lower panel starts in 2025–26 because the held-back tonnage cannot be computed for the filed years: for a facility already on the floor, the register publishes the floored number and not the one behind it. The projection holds production and emissions intensity fixed. It does not model the separate convergence of facility-specific intensity toward the industry average, which would push more facilities onto the floor, not fewer — so 45 is a conservative count. Facilities that have ceased to be covered are excluded throughout: the floor holds up a number for them, but not an obligation.

Source: Clean Energy Regulator published Safeguard baselines and emissions, FY2023–24 and FY2024–25 (208 covered facilities on 209 rows in the later year, from a 228-row table). Analysis by Heliovulcan.

Those nineteen are the whole of the group that emits less than its floored baseline — one of them under 300 tonnes against a 100,000-tonne allowance. They are inert, but they are inert outside the scheme, and a tighter floor would not reach them. The 45 counted above are the ones it would.

For a covered facility on the floor, the baseline is the coverage threshold itself, and the only compliant position is outside the scheme.

The cohort is concentrated in a few sectors. Of the 45 covered facilities that reach the floor by 2029–30, 17 are metal ore mines, 10 are coal mines and 5 are oil and gas facilities. Metal ore mining is the sector that stands out: it is 24 per cent of the covered register and 38 per cent of the floor cohort. Coal runs the other way — 32 per cent of the register, 22 per cent of the cohort — because coal mines are, on average, larger emitters and stay above the floor. What the floor catches is the small end of a big industry: the satellite pit, the ageing operation, the plant in care and maintenance.

Why the threshold question is a floor question

Because the minimum baseline moves with the coverage threshold, the choice the department is consulting on is not only about admissions. It is also about whether the 45 start declining again.

Exhibit 4 Most of the effect of a lower threshold lands on facilities that are already covered
Baseline no longer granted to facilities already covered Emissions newly brought into the scheme

The two bars are the same unit and not the same kind of thing — that is the point of the chart. The teal bar is baseline withdrawn: every tonne of it is an abatement obligation that lands the year the change takes effect. The orange bar is emissions brought inside the scheme, of which only the part above a newly issued baseline is ever an obligation — on the department’s own Option 2 for new entrants, close to none of it in the first year. Read that way, the smaller bar is doing more work than the larger one.

The two right-hand columns are the department’s; the two left-hand ones are not published anywhere. “Baseline held above the declining path” is the sum, over floor-bound facilities, of the difference between the statutory minimum and the projected unfloored baseline.

Read the rows against each other. Moving the threshold from 100,000 to 75,000 tonnes admits 48 facilities carrying 4.1 Mt of emissions — but those emissions are not abatement. A newly covered facility gets a baseline, and only the gap between its emissions and that baseline is an obligation. Under the department’s own Option 2, where new entrants start near the top of the decline curve, the obligation in the first year is close to nothing.

The floor column is different in kind. It is 0.92 Mt of baseline that stops being granted, to facilities that are already regulated, already reporting, and already covered. It requires no new production variables, no new administrative machinery and no lead time. It is the cheapest tonne in the section, and it is not in the table.

Three things have to be said about that 0.92 Mt, because they cut in different directions.

It is a lower bound. For the 24 covered facilities already parked on the floor, the published baseline of 100,000 is already the floored number — the true calculated baseline behind it is lower, by an amount the register does not disclose. Projecting 100,000 forward therefore overstates their unfloored 2029 baseline, which understates the held-back tonnage. The real figure is larger.

The step from 75,000 to 50,000 matters more than it looks. Because 24 covered facilities are parked on the identical number, they project to an identical number — 72,838 tonnes in 2029–30. A 75,000-tonne floor leaves them floored, just barely. A 50,000-tonne floor releases them entirely. The relationship between threshold and effect is a cliff, and where the cliff sits depends on undisclosed data.

None of this is an argument that lower is better. The department’s own reasoning against a 25,000-tonne threshold — 291 new facilities, 18 new industry groups, administrative cost outrunning abatement — is sound and this analysis does not touch it. The point is narrower: whatever threshold is chosen, its largest single effect in the first year will fall on facilities that are already covered, and that effect should be in the consultation table.

Most identifiable onsite abatement activity is concentrated in mining

Both the department and the Authority describe the same worry in almost the same words. Net emissions are falling fast — 7.4 per cent then 5.5 per cent across the two post-reform years. Gross emissions, the ones that actually come out of the stack, are falling at 1.9 and 2.4 per cent. The difference is being filled with credits. The Authority puts it plainly: material on-site abatement “appears limited to a few facilities”.

Neither paper says where. The register does.

Four-fifths of offset-credit surrender comes from three resource sectors: coal mining 48 per cent, oil and gas extraction 18 per cent and metal ore mining 15 per cent of the 10.5 million credits surrendered in FY2024–25. Coal alone surrenders more than every non-resource sector put together. The sector chart and totals, in the appendix →

Of the 24 facilities that surrendered offset credits equal to 30 per cent or more of their baseline — the threshold at which the regulator requires a written explanation of why more was not done on site — 12 are coal mines and 10 are metal ore mines. Twenty-two of twenty-four.

So the measures on the table — quantitative limits on credit use, discounting so that more than one credit is needed per tonne, vintage restrictions — are, whatever their stated framing, instruments aimed at mining. That is worth naming before the design is settled, because the assumption underneath all three is that the party holding the baseline can respond by investing on site. For a large and identifiable class of mines, it cannot.

The incentive and the owner

At an off-grid mine, on-site abatement mostly means one thing: changing what the power station burns, or putting solar and storage in front of it. That is the only lever of any scale, and at most sites the mine does not hold it.

We have gone through the Australian gold sector site by site — 88 operations carrying a Safeguard or pollutant-inventory identity, with station ownership, storage and contract expiry established from contractor project registers, state generation licences and company filings rather than assumed. None of those three facts appears in any national register.

Of the sites verified as having their own power station, 90 per cent do not own it. It belongs to a contractor — typically one of about seven fund-owned independent power producers — under a build-own-operate contract with a term measured in a decade or more. Two operations own their station outright.

The consequences run in a straight line:

  • Every disclosed battery at those sites sits on the contractor’s asset. Fifteen of the operations have storage; in each case it was installed by the power provider inside an existing contract, not procured by the miner.
  • The carbon and the asset are on different balance sheets. The mine reports the generation emissions and holds the baseline. The contractor owns the plant, chooses the fuel, and captures the fuel saving first.
  • The date the decision reopens is almost never public. Contract expiry is established for nine of the 88 sites. For the rest, nobody outside the two counterparties has looked.
Exhibit 7 The baseline and the power station are on different balance sheets

This is the structure a credit price signal has to travel through. It reaches the left box, where the obligation is. The decision it is meant to trigger — change the fuel, add solar, add storage — is made in the right box, by a counterparty whose contract does not price carbon and typically has years left to run.

Source: Heliovulcan site-by-site build over 88 Australian gold operations carrying a Safeguard or pollutant-inventory identity, from contractor project registers, state generation licences and company filings. None of the three facts — station ownership, storage, contract expiry — appears in any national register. Percentages exclude 22 sites recorded as not yet checked; a blank storage field means no disclosure was found, never that no battery exists.

Discounting credits raises the price of compliance for the party holding the baseline. At most off-grid mines, the asset that would answer belongs to somebody else, on a contract with years to run.

This is a split incentive; a higher offset price leaves it in place. Making offsets dearer raises the cost on the miner and leaves the investment decision where it already sits — with a contractor whose economics improve when fuel is saved but who did not sign up to a carbon obligation. The Authority’s question 8 asks how on-site abatement should be measured; question 10 asks whether stronger incentives are warranted. The prior question is who the incentive lands on, and for this class of facility the answer is published nowhere and is knowable only site by site.

The same caution applies, for a different reason, to coal: tightening coal baselines creates a compliance cost at mines that are all grid-connected, and no generation-side market. Why, in the appendix →

Where a lower threshold would land

The consultation paper says the biggest increase in facility numbers under a lower threshold is in metal ore mining and oil and gas extraction. It does not say what those facilities look like operationally, because the department is counting emissions and we are looking at power stations.

Four hundred and thirty-three Australian mines sit outside the Safeguard register today. Two hundred and forty-five of them are operating. Of those, 98 are off-grid — 49 gold and silver operations, 19 iron ore, the balance spread across mineral sands, base metals and bauxite. A further 54 sit on a weak connection.

Whether any individual one crosses a 75,000 or 50,000-tonne threshold depends on emissions we cannot compute from outside, and this analysis does not claim to identify them. What it does establish is the character of the population: a lower threshold does not mainly admit factories. It admits remote sites that make their own electricity, where the abatement lever is a physical power station with a fuel bill and a contract, and where — on the gold evidence above — the odds are that the station belongs to a third party.

For anyone whose business is selling into that transition, that is the material fact. A threshold change converts a discretionary cost decision at an off-grid power station into a regulated one. It does not change the physics or the capital cost. It changes who has to have the conversation, and when.

Three disciplines for reading a coverage proposal

Three disciplines follow. Read the mechanism itself: the largest quantified consequence in the coverage section is one uncosted sentence about the minimum baseline, visible only once the calculation is rebuilt. Ask who the instrument lands on before asking whether it is strong enough: at most off-grid mines the generating asset belongs to a contractor on a decade-long contract. Separate the compliance cost from the addressable market: tightening coal baselines is real money and almost no generation-side opportunity. The three disciplines in full →
