Technical appendix

How the baseline-floor analysis was built

Method, reconciliation against the department’s published figures, limitations and the data tables behind Lowering the Safeguard threshold also tightens baselines for facilities already covered.

The three review documents

The three documents are one review seen from three angles, and each hands work to the next.

The Carbon Leakage Review, led by Frank Jotzo and published in February, closed with a recommendation to introduce a border carbon adjustment for cement and clinker first, to consider lime, hydrogen and ammonia, steel and iron, and glass later, to refuse export rebates outright, and — the part that matters here — to remove concessional trade-exposed baselines for any commodity once a border adjustment covers it. Its recommendations were handed to the Safeguard review.

The Climate Change Authority was given a deliberately narrow brief: the post-2030 baseline decline rate, and the extent to which the reforms are actually driving abatement at the facilities themselves. Its preliminary arithmetic puts a decline rate of about 4.5 per cent a year against the bottom of Australia’s 2035 target range and about 7 per cent against the top — against an indicative 3.285 per cent currently written into the rules. It says explicitly that the coverage threshold and international units are outside its scope.

The department’s consultation paper is the wide one. It carries the decline rate, credit restrictions, trade-exposed concessions, the border adjustment, banking and borrowing, coal baselines, road-versus-rail coverage, and the coverage threshold. It opens by stating that the review “is not intended to make fundamental reforms” and that changes will generally take effect from 1 July 2030.

Within that, the coverage threshold gets a short section and a clean table: drop from 100,000 tonnes to 75,000 and 48 more facilities come in, carrying 4.1 Mt of newly covered emissions; drop to 50,000 and it is 108 facilities and 7.8 Mt. The biggest increases, the paper notes, are in metal ore mining and oil and gas extraction.

Method, reconciliation and limitations

How this was done

An independent rebuild of the baseline calculation, checked against the department’s own published figures

No table publishes the floor analysis; it has to be rebuilt. It comes from an independent reconstruction of the Safeguard baseline calculation from the rule text and the published register, each facility’s baseline rescaled by the legislated emissions reduction contribution from 0.902 to 0.657, then tested against the statutory minimum at four candidate thresholds.

Covered facilities are identified from the published table’s own note that a facility is an eligible facility under section 58B, not from its emissions. The FY2024–25 table carries 228 rows; 19 are eligible facilities that have dropped below the coverage threshold and remain listed. The remaining 209 rows are the Regulator’s 208 covered facilities (Telfer Gold Mine is on two rows after a mid-year change of operator) with 132,797,928 tonnes of covered emissions, which reconciles exactly with the Regulator’s published totals. One covered row has no production variable and therefore no baseline; it cannot sit on the floor and drops out of the floor counts. Every floor figure in this article is on that basis.

The reconstruction is validated by reproducing figures the department published from its own data. Offset credits surrendered come out at 10,831,682 against the paper’s 10.8 million; scheme credits at 2,582,112 against 2.6 million; coal mining at 68 facilities and 31.8 Mt against the paper’s “around a third of covered facilities” and 31.7 Mt. Facilities surrendering credits above 30 per cent of baseline come out at 24 against the paper’s 23 — a definitional edge on deemed surrenders, and the one number we do not match exactly.

Correction, 10 August 2026. As first published, this article counted the floor cohort over all 227 rows of the published table rather than the 208 covered facilities, which put 19 facilities that had already left the scheme inside the cohort. The FY2029–30 figures were 64 facilities and 1.51 Mt; they are 45 and 0.99 Mt. The tightening a 75,000-tonne threshold would deliver was 1.4 Mt; it is 0.92 Mt. The sentence describing floor-bound facilities as facing no declining constraint was wrong in the other direction, and has been replaced: a covered facility on the floor has no headroom at all. The mechanism, the exhibits’ construction and the offset-credit figures are unchanged.

The ownership evidence is a separate build and a slower one. Station ownership, storage and contract expiry exist in no national register, so each is either established from a named public source or recorded as unchecked. Forty-four of the 88 gold sites are verified; 22 are explicitly not yet checked and are excluded from the percentages rather than assumed. A blank storage field means no disclosure was found, never that no battery exists — behind-the-meter storage has no register in Australia, and reading a blank as a zero has put a site with nine megawatts of installed battery at the top of a target list before now.

Section 56(4) in the register

The band between the two baselines is still there and still pays nothing. But a covered facility cannot sit inside it. Emissions in that band are emissions below 100,000 tonnes — below the coverage threshold — so a facility arrives there by leaving the scheme, not by abating within it. Nineteen facilities in the 2024–25 table have done exactly that: still listed, still showing a baseline of 100,000, emitting below it, and carrying no obligation because they are no longer designated large facilities. One of them was issued 1,138 credits against emissions of 81,563 tonnes — measured, that is, against an unfloored baseline of 82,701 rather than the 100,000 the table prints. That is section 56(4) working exactly as drafted, and it is the one place in the register where you can watch it happen.

Where a lower floor would bite

Exhibit 5 Where a lower floor would bite: twenty-three covered facilities land on the same number
Below the current 100,000 t floor — the cohort Above it — still declining normally

This is why the relationship between the threshold and its effect is a cliff rather than a slope. Twenty-four covered facilities are filed at exactly 100,000 tonnes today, so they project to exactly the same number in 2029–30 — and they sit just under 75,000. A 75,000-tonne floor leaves almost all of them floored; a 50,000-tonne floor releases every one. No covered facility projects below 50,000 tonnes. The 116 facilities above 150,000 tonnes are off the right of the chart and are nowhere near the floor. The distribution above 100,000 tonnes is unchanged by the coverage filter — every facility it removes sits below the floor.

Coal baselines and the generation-side market

The same caution applies, for a different reason, to coal. Both papers raise ending the concession that lets existing coal mines keep half of their baseline on facility-specific intensity past 2030 while everyone else converges to the industry average. Doing so would raise the liability on gassy mines — correctly, on the scheme’s own logic. But of 102 Australian coal mines in our screen, none is off-grid, and the emissions at issue are ventilation air methane and haul fleet diesel. Tightening coal baselines creates a compliance cost. It does not create a generation-side market, and any business case built on the assumption that it does will be wrong by an order of magnitude.

Three disciplines for reading a coverage proposal, in full

Three disciplines follow. Read the mechanism itself — the largest quantified consequence in the coverage section of a 48-page consultation paper is one uncosted sentence about the minimum baseline, and it is only visible if you have rebuilt the calculation. Ask who the instrument lands on before asking whether it is strong enough — credit discounting is aimed at the party holding the baseline, and at most off-grid mines the generating asset belongs to a contractor on a decade-long contract, so the price signal and the investment decision sit on different balance sheets. Separate the compliance cost from the addressable market — tightening coal baselines is real money and almost no generation-side opportunity, because the emissions are ventilation methane and haul fleet, not a power station. For the adjacent sizing question, see most of the diesel is on wheels; for what the ownership work looks like at site level, see how the analysis works.
A rule with a floor in it does not stop a facility decarbonising. It stops the scheme asking.

Assumptions The projection holds production and emissions intensity fixed and rescales the baseline by the emissions reduction contribution alone. It does not model the convergence of facility-specific intensity toward the industry average, which on a single tested facility understated the projected gap by 23–37 per cent; it does not model borrowing adjustments, which the floor provision expressly carves out. Both omissions bias the floor count downward.
Limitations For facilities already at the statutory minimum, the published baseline is the floored figure, so their true unfloored baseline is unknown and the held-back tonnage reported here is a lower bound. The department’s admissions figures in Exhibit 4 are reproduced from its consultation paper and have not been independently rebuilt; we cannot compute sub-threshold facility emissions from public data and do not identify which facilities a lower threshold would admit. The gold ownership percentages exclude 22 sites recorded as not yet checked. No facility is named in this article as a commercial target.
