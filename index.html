Existing operations

Understanding an industrial operation

Before assessing a new project at a mine or industrial plant, it helps to establish how the existing operation works. What does the operation burn, buy and emit? What compliance obligations does it face, and who controls its energy supply?

Energy

What it burns and what it buys

Fuel mix and quantity, self-generation versus purchased electricity, reconstructed average load, and network position. We establish the fuel type from evidence rather than assuming it from the industry code, which is wrong often enough to matter.

Control

Who can actually decide

Ownership and operator structure. This often matters: the power station frequently belongs to a third party under a long-term contract, and its fuel is reported under a different industry entirely.

Position

Where it sits in its sector

We compare the asset with similar operations, then check whether the facility-level results add up to the published sector total. Differences between the two can reveal classification, reporting or scope errors.

A common error in 2029 Safeguard projections

Many projections apply the annual decline rate and continue below the statutory baseline floor. That can materially overstate a facility’s future compliance gap. In one facility we tested, the difference was almost sevenfold.

Project analysis

Screening a proposed project

This is the part of the work with the longest track record. It applies when someone is proposing something new: a mine microgrid, a diesel replacement, a solar-plus-storage concept. The output is an early view of whether the project should advance, together with a list of information to request from the owner. It is not a forecast or an investment recommendation.

We use public and early project information to test four things: approvals, site constraints, the electrical system and the commercial case. The result shows whether the project is ready for further work and what information is still needed. See how the screen works →

How the work is built

Where the information comes from

Every analysis starts from the same public records, linked so that one operation can be placed against its company, its network and its sector. How the analysis works sets out the sources, the checks and the evidence labels.

Around 8,000 industrial sites are mapped as background context, which is how we place a single operation against its network, its neighbours and its sector. Mapping a site does not mean it has been analysed in depth: that smaller count, and what it covers, is on the record page. Neither is a database you can query.

Heliovulcan is an independent research practice led by Grant Chen, an energy consultant and modeller working across industrial energy, emissions and project analysis.

Grant’s background includes national and facility-level emissions analysis, industrial decarbonisation, government policy, and early-stage screening of energy projects. Client and project names are kept confidential where required, but the methods and the public-data work are published here.

The work helps identify important gaps before detailed engineering or formal due diligence begins. It does not replace engineering, environmental studies, legal advice or formal due diligence.

You do not need any of this to start a conversation, and a research, sector or policy question needs none of it at all. But for a specific site, five things make the first read much sharper: site type; annual electricity use or diesel consumption (approximate is fine); current energy or fuel cost; rough load pattern; and the decision you are actually facing.
