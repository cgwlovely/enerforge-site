Screening energy options for weak-grid and off-grid mines

Project insight 26 June 2026 7-minute read Research note

← All research Discuss a question

A weak-grid or off-grid mine buys reliability as well as energy. The served load is the part of site demand the proposed system is expected to supply, and the sites where a hybrid system makes the most sense are often the ones a lowest-cost or current-load screen misreads. This note sets out the seven questions to settle before a system is designed: the load that can be served reliably, how quickly power is needed, what diesel is doing today, what the connection route risks, whether mine life supports the tenor, where the commercial boundary sits, and when a feasibility study is worth commissioning.

1 · Reliability and the load the system will serve

Screening a mine-site project like a commercial rooftop, as annual energy times a tariff less the cost of the system, produces a misleading number. A commercial building can often assess electricity primarily as an operating cost. At a remote mine, power reliability is also a production constraint, so the cost of interruption has to be included in the energy decision. When the plant stops, the loss is measured in lost production per hour, not cents per kWh. That single difference reorders the whole problem.

A common early-stage error is to design for the maximum renewable share while assuming away the operating role diesel still performs. A screen that respects operational continuity looks different from the first line.

Mines and heavy industrial sites often run peak loads several times their average, driven by intermittent high-draw equipment. Sizing a solar and battery system to meet those rare peaks raises capital cost while utilisation falls, and the economics fail before a contract is drafted. The better approach is to size for the sustained, predictable load: average demand, night load and baseload consumption. Diesel keeps the peaks. The BESS is asked to displace a meaningful, contractable share of diesel energy, and to do it every day.

The screen therefore turns on one question:

Which part of site demand can be served repeatedly, reliably and under contract?

2 · Time-to-power

Paces’ starting point is a sobering development statistic: only a minority of projects taken into a utility-scale pipeline ultimately reach construction. Many projects that look viable on a spreadsheet fail later on grid cost, permitting, land or community constraints — after substantial time and money have already been committed.

The useful lesson is sequencing. Traditional development often spends deeply before the most dangerous assumptions have been tested. A better process scores completion risk early and advances only sites with a credible pathway through interconnection, approvals and procurement.

The most valuable early work is usually not making the model more precise. It is finding the reason the project may never be built.

For fast-growing loads such as data centres, a grid offer several years away may have little practical value. The opportunity cost of delay can exceed the premium for building dedicated generation beside the load. That pushes projects behind the meter and changes the comparison from which option has the lowest standalone cost of energy to which option can deliver reliable power within the required time.

Remote mines have always faced a version of the same decision. They build power because extending or strengthening the grid is slow, expensive or impossible. What is changing is that the same logic is now being validated by large, capital-rich energy users.

3 · Diesel’s operating roles

Diesel generation at a remote mine is doing several jobs simultaneously, and a model that sees only the first one will mis-size everything downstream:

  • Energy supply — the baseload kilowatt-hours the site runs on.
  • Capacity reserve — firm capacity available on demand, regardless of weather.
  • Backup — cover for solar variability and equipment outages.
  • Peak support — the buffer for mill start-ups, hoist cycles and compressor surges.
  • Contingency supply — power during rare or extended shortfalls.

Only the first of those is straightforward to displace with PV and storage. The others are why the site keeps diesel on even when it is expensive. A design that assumes diesel can simply be removed misreads what the customer is buying. The practical question is which part of the load can be served reliably and economically by solar and storage, while diesel stays available for peaks and contingencies.

The operating architecture that follows coordinates energy, fast response and firm capacity around the production load, with a defined control boundary:

Solar PVLow-cost daytime energy
BESSShifting, ramps, peaks, reserve
Diesel / gasDeep firming and contingency
Microgrid controllerDispatch, protection and stability
Mine loadProduction and critical services
Grid / future optionImport, export or later connection

Reliability is a binding constraint. The controller must make the portfolio behave as one power station.

The Paces off-grid analysis points to a familiar cost curve: high renewable shares can remain competitive when firming is retained, while the final move toward 100% renewable supply becomes disproportionately expensive. The system must then overbuild generation and storage to cover rare low-resource periods.

For a mine or industrial site, the practical architecture is therefore not “battery instead of engines”. It is:

  • a renewable block carrying a large share of annual energy;
  • BESS providing shifting, ramp control, peak support and short-duration firming;
  • diesel or gas generation retained for deep lulls, contingencies and reliability;
  • a future grid connection, longer-duration asset or additional renewable block treated as an option rather than a prerequisite.

The final increment towards 100% renewable supply can require disproportionate generation, storage and backup capacity. The preferred share is therefore a reliability and capital decision, not only an energy-cost decision.

4 · Grid connection risk

  • Queue position is not usable capacity. A project can wait behind speculative applications and still receive an uneconomic connection outcome.
  • Upgrade cost arrives late. A transformer, substation or protection requirement can destroy the economics after the site has already absorbed development spend.
  • Grid conditions change while the project waits. Available capacity can be consumed by other projects, and assumptions made at origination may not survive the study process.
  • Planning risk can move in the opposite direction. Areas with attractive grid capacity may attract concentrated development and trigger local opposition or new restrictions.

Development schedules can be lost in procurement as easily as in permitting. Long-lead transformers, switchgear, PCS, battery containers and generation equipment may decide when a project can energise. A credible time-to-power plan therefore needs supplier engagement, lead-time evidence, deposit assumptions and interface ownership early in development.

5 · Mine life

Remaining mine life sets the tenor over which any energy asset and its contract can be recovered. A hybrid system with a fifteen-year design life at a mine with six years of reserves is either a short-tenor contract with a residual-value problem, or a redeployable asset, or a case that does not close. Mine life and the expiry of the existing power contract are public for most operations; fleet age and load data are not. The screen therefore checks mine life and contract term first, and asks the owner for the rest.

6 · Commercial boundary

Once you accept that the system has to protect continuity, the reliability specification stops being a technical detail and starts driving the commercials. What the mine cannot afford to lose — and for how long — flows straight into:

  • BESS duration and how much autonomy is really required
  • PCS sizing and whether grid-forming capability is needed
  • Redundancy (N+1) and spinning-reserve assumptions
  • O&M intensity and availability guarantees
  • CAPEX — and therefore the debt the cashflow has to carry

The technical question and the financing question are inseparable. A system can solve the reliability problem and still produce a debt case that does not close, because the reliability spec pushed CAPEX past what the contractable diesel saving can service. Sizing must be driven by what the commercial structure can support — not by the maximum renewable share the engineers can reach.

Defining the load slice that can be served reliably and contractually sets the boundary the whole commercial case lives inside. The diesel saving inside that boundary has to become a contracted cashflow before it is project revenue; how that conversion works is set out in From customer savings to financeable BESS cash flow.

7 · When to proceed to feasibility

  1. Development risk is part of the product. The value of Pre-DD is surfacing approval, site, grid, boundary and financeability gaps before formal development spend.
  2. Off-grid hybrid is an economic pathway in its own right. For remote and constrained sites it can provide a faster, self-controlled route to power while preserving reliability.
  3. Schedule and equipment belong in the first client conversation. A technically attractive design without a credible procurement and energisation pathway is not development-ready.

A project moves from screen to feasibility study when the served-load boundary is defined, the connection or islanded route is chosen on time-to-power grounds, mine life and contract term support the tenor, and the owner has supplied load, diesel and reliability data. Until then, a feasibility study would be proving a design that may be answering the wrong question. The screen itself is set out in A Pre-DD screen is not a feasibility study and the F1–F4 methodology.
