What makes a BESS project development-ready?

A battery project is ready when its site control, planning approval, grid connection, revenue, delivery structure and financing are all in place, and when responsibility for every interface between them is clear. Here, development-ready means that the project has no unresolved issue likely to stop construction or financing. These five notes take the question one layer at a time. The series combines published industry sources with lessons from our screening work. It is not a representative sample of Australian projects.

Submission · DCCEEW · September 2026

Set the post-2030 decline rate and the trade-exposed pathway together

The Department can reproduce the starting point from public facility data. Using the published register, we calculate a starting decline rate of 3.53% a year for the 62% target and 5.99% for the 70% target under current rules. If the rate for trade-exposed facilities falls by one percentage point, the other 187 facilities must reduce by about 0.2 percentage points more. Many rate combinations meet the same target; policy determines how the burden is shared.

The question addressed

The consultation paper states that the decline rate for 2030-31 to 2034-35 must be calibrated to meet the agreed target, and lists the factors that calibration has to take into account: the target, production at existing facilities, facility entry and exit, uptake of concessional baselines, policy changes from the review, the reserve and credits issued to below-threshold facilities. The published estimates (RepuTex, EY and Australian Energy Producers) give different ranges for the final rate, and their published methods generally do not separate the mechanical effect of the Rule from assumptions about production, abatement, entry and exit. This submission separates the result produced by the Rule from later assumptions about production, abatement, new facilities and closures. It gives a rule-based starting point from public data, the adjustments that connect it to a final rate, and a way to test those adjustments after implementation.

Key findings

  1. The default decline rate and the post-2030 trade-exposed pathway must be calibrated together.

    For a given 2035 target, each combination of the two rates that meets the target lies on one iso-target curve. Reducing the trade-exposed rate by one percentage point a year increases the default rate required of the other 187 facilities by approximately 0.2 points a year.

  2. Under current rules the rule-based starting point is 3.53% a year for the 62% target and 5.99% for the 70% target.

    The starting point is calculated from the FY2024-25 register, the Rule’s own formulas, published commodity projections and a limited set of stated scenario conventions, and can be reproduced from the inputs listed in the submission. At the legislated indicative rate of 3.285% the starting point reaches FY2034-35 net emissions of 70.25 Mt, 1.4 Mt above the 62% point target of 68.8 Mt.

  3. The difference from the Department’s projection of 66 Mt comes from stated assumptions rather than from the Rule.

    The Department’s projection assumes that facilities change their behaviour, including by reducing emissions onsite. Our rule-based starting point assumes no additional onsite abatement. Annex B compares the two series year by year and shows how assumptions about production, new facilities, closures and abatement explain the difference.

  4. Publicly visible new entrants would add about 17.0 Mt a year of covered emissions against 2.59 Mt of baseline once all operate.

    The pipeline is 41 coal mines, 5 LNG trains and 16 iron ore mines in public trackers, entered on a phased profile. The calibration should list large known projects separately, including the off-grid data-centre proposal examined in Annex F, instead of including them in a general growth assumption.

  5. The annual register can test the main assumptions.

    The Department should publish how emissions intensity changed across existing facilities, by industry (Annex G). That measure shows whether onsite abatement is tracking the path assumed in the calibration. The Department should also state in advance when it would recalibrate the rate.

  6. The covered population is 208 facilities.

    The published register has 228 rows. Nineteen of those rows are eligible facilities under Rule s 58B, which are facilities still listed after falling below the coverage threshold. Removing them leaves 209 rows, and Telfer Gold Mine occupies two of them after a mid-year change of operator. Covered emissions are 132.8 Mt on either count. The Safeguard Atlas recomputes the submission’s published figures in the browser from the same inputs.

Recommendation

Adopt and publish a framework for post-2030 calibration that others can reproduce. Use three published steps and review them regularly.

  1. Establish the starting point

    Set the default rate and the trade-exposed rate together, either recomputed by the Department on the same assumptions or taken from the rule-based starting point in this submission. State whether the compliance condition is the FY2034–35 point target or the five-year budget.

  2. Quantify the adjustments

    Before the period begins, publish the effect of each adjustment separately: production, entry and exit, trade-exposed treatment, onsite abatement, below-threshold credits and the reserve. State how much each one moves the rate, and list large publicly identified projects individually.

  3. Monitor outcomes and recalibrate

    Publish the relative-intensity effect of covered facilities with each annual register, compare it with the abatement pathway used in the calibration, and set out the recalibration mechanism in advance.

Open questions

Annex E lists the information that is not publicly available and identifies who is likely to hold it. We do not estimate the missing values. Instead, we state the rule used for each scenario.

  • Post-2030 production at existing facilities.
  • The commissioning probability and timing of new projects.
  • Closures of non-coal facilities beyond the four confirmed.
  • Trade-exposed applications and renewals after 2030.
  • Policy changes arising from the review.
  • Credits issued to below-threshold facilities.
  • The size and composition of the reserve.

Methods

How we build and check the analysis

We rebuild each asset account from its own records, check it against independent data and label every figure as reported, calculated or assumed.

Step 1

1. Reconstruct the asset

We rebuild the asset from regulatory filings, licences and company disclosures: its energy use, fuel, emissions, production and costs, and who owns and operates each part.

Step 2

2. Test it against independent data

We compare the result with an independently assembled account, such as national energy statistics, a pollutant inventory or trade data. We investigate and report any material difference.

Two figures only confirm each other if they come from separate sources. A facility’s reported emissions and the regulator’s published intensity factor will always agree, because one is calculated from the other. Comparing them proves nothing, and it is an easy mistake to make. Every cross-check we report names both sets of inputs and shows that they do not overlap.

Step 3

3. Separate reported, calculated and assumed values

Regulatory filing Reported by the operator to a regulator. Usually the strongest public source, but still checked for unit and scope differences.

How this is calculated

The calculation applies the same three steps as the Safeguard Rule to every facility in every year.

Step one — the baseline. The emissions reduction contribution, or ERC, is a multiplier that reduces most baselines each year. Under Rule s 11, an existing facility's baseline is its production multiplied by an applicable emissions intensity and by that multiplier. The ERC falls each year: 0.902 in FY2024-25, then 0.049 lower each year to 0.657 in FY2029-30, and from FY2030-31 by the post-2030 decline rate selected in the policy section. Because the ERC is a multiplier, each published baseline is scaled by the change in its ERC, because facility-level production and intensity are not public. The 21 facilities with a trade-exposed determination follow the ERC path the Regulator has published for each of them. The treatment of those facilities after their determinations expire is a policy setting, not an input.

Step two — the minimum baseline. Under Rule s 10(1), a baseline that calculates below 100,000 t CO₂-e is set at 100,000 t. The credit issuance formula in Rule s 57 uses the baseline that would apply if s 10(1) had not been made. The model therefore uses the minimum baseline for compliance but not for calculating credits.

Step three — the compliance measure. Modelled net emissions for a facility are the smaller of its covered emissions and its baseline. The national figure is the sum across facilities. Production is applied as one factor per commodity, so a coal mine follows the coal production path and an LNG plant follows the LNG path.

How to read these charts, and how they are calculated

The iso-target curve. Each point on the curve shows a pair of decline rates that reaches the same 2035 target. For a fixed target, each post-2030 decline rate for the 21 facilities with a trade-exposed determination implies a single rate for the remaining facilities at which the national total meets the target exactly. Plotting that implied rate for each value gives the curve. Every point on the curve meets the same target; the points differ in how the reduction is distributed between the two groups. Points above the curve exceed the target and points below it fall short.

Interpretation. Where the curve crosses the dashed 45° line, both groups decline at the same rate. That is the single-rate result, and it is the quantity the consultation question asks about. Moving to the left along the curve reduces the rate required of the facilities with a determination; the curve gives the corresponding increase required of the other 187 facilities. Because the two groups differ greatly in size, the exchange is far from one-for-one.

Solution method. The rate is found by repeated halving of the search range, to a precision of about 0.0001 percentage points; each step recalculates all 209 facility rows, and no fitted parameters are used. The full method is on the method page.

Effect of additional onsite abatement. Modelled net emissions are the smaller of covered emissions and the baseline. At first, additional abatement reduces the credits a facility must surrender. It reduces modelled net emissions only after the facility's emissions fall below its baseline. On the starting-point assumptions, net emissions therefore begin to fall only when the abatement rate is large enough to bring facilities below their baselines.

A lower Safeguard threshold would also tighten baselines for existing facilities

Quantitative analysis 7 August 2026 11-minute read Climate policy · Industrial assets · Australia

← All research Discuss a question

Debate about a lower Safeguard threshold has focused on the facilities that would enter the scheme. The threshold also sets the minimum baseline for facilities already covered.

Lowering the threshold from 100,000 to 75,000 tonnes would reduce their aggregate FY2029–30 baseline by at least 0.92 Mt CO2-e. The consultation paper quantifies the 4.1 Mt of emissions that would enter coverage, but not this separate effect on facilities already covered.

Question
How would a lower coverage threshold affect facilities already inside the Safeguard Mechanism?
Basis
The FY2024–25 and FY2023–24 Safeguard registers; the Safeguard Rule ss 10(1), 11, 56(4) and 58B; the consultation paper’s table 3.3; Heliovulcan’s dataset covering 88 gold operations.
Method
The analysis projects each covered facility’s published baseline to FY2029–30 using the legislated emissions reduction contribution. It then applies minimum baselines of 100,000, 75,000, 50,000 and 25,000 tonnes and compares the resulting aggregate baselines. Coverage status is taken from the register’s section 58B disclosure, and national totals are reconciled with the department’s published estimates.

Twenty-four covered facilities currently sit on the 100,000-tonne minimum baseline; 45 are projected to reach it by FY2029–30. Reducing the threshold to 75,000 tonnes would lower the aggregate baseline for existing covered facilities by at least 0.92 Mt CO2-e a year. This is separate from the 4.1 Mt of emissions associated with newly covered facilities.

The model holds facility production and emissions intensity constant apart from the legislated decline factor. Public data do not provide emissions for individual sub-threshold facilities. For facilities already on the minimum baseline, the register does not disclose the lower calculated value behind it, so the estimated baseline reduction is a lower bound.

The threshold changes two parts of the scheme

Three current policy processes address related parts of the Safeguard Mechanism.

  • The Carbon Leakage Review considers border adjustment and trade-exposed baselines.
  • The Climate Change Authority considers the post-2030 decline rate and onsite abatement.
  • The department’s consultation paper considers the coverage threshold and other scheme settings.

This analysis addresses one interaction in the department’s paper: the relationship between the coverage threshold and the statutory minimum baseline. The paper’s threshold table counts the newly covered facilities: 75,000 tonnes admits 48 facilities carrying 4.1 Mt of emissions, 50,000 tonnes admits 108 facilities and 7.8 Mt, and 25,000 tonnes admits 291 facilities and 14.2 Mt, with the largest increases in metal ore mining and oil and gas extraction. Section 3.5.1 states that the minimum baseline would fall with the coverage threshold:

“Under either approach, the minimum baseline — currently 100,000 tonnes — would fall to be consistent with the new coverage threshold.”

Safeguard Mechanism Review Consultation Paper, August 2026, section 3.5.1

The consultation paper quantifies the emissions and facilities that would enter the scheme, but it does not quantify the resulting reduction in baselines for facilities already covered. The reconstruction below indicates that the effect is material.

How the minimum baseline works

The Safeguard Rule uses different baseline values for compliance obligations and credit issuance. Section 10(1) prevents a covered facility’s compliance baseline from falling below 100,000 tonnes. Section 56(4), however, measures credit issuance against the lower baseline that would have applied without that floor.

Compliance obligation. A facility’s baseline is set to 100,000 tonnes if the number calculated under the ordinary rules comes out below 100,000.

National Greenhouse and Energy Reporting (Safeguard Mechanism) Rule 2015, s10(1)

Credit issuance. Credits are issued against the baseline that would have been calculated “if subsection 10(1) had not been enacted” — that is, against the unfloored number.

Same instrument, s56(4)

The two provisions serve different purposes. The minimum baseline limits the compliance burden on smaller or declining facilities. Using the unfloored baseline for credit issuance prevents the same facilities from receiving credits solely because their compliance baseline has been held at the minimum.

For a facility that remains covered while sitting on the minimum baseline, reported emissions will generally exceed its compliance baseline: the facility is covered because its emissions exceed 100,000 tonnes, and its baseline, once floored, is 100,000 tonnes. In FY2024–25, all 24 covered facilities on the 100,000-tonne minimum reported emissions above that level. The lowest reported 101,144 tonnes and the median 127,011 tonnes.

The other 19 facilities shown at 100,000 tonnes were no longer designated large facilities for the year and therefore carried no Safeguard obligation. They are excluded from the estimated effect of changing the minimum baseline; the register evidence for them, including one credited against its unfloored baseline under section 56(4), is in the technical appendix.

Falling below the threshold does not always end coverage: a facility inside a multi-year monitoring period stays in, and one facility in the 2024–25 table is covered on emissions of 80,908 tonnes on that basis. It is the only such case in the table.

The register shows the covered group on the minimum baseline growing year by year.

Moving the threshold from 100,000 to 75,000 tonnes admits 48 facilities carrying 4.1 Mt of emissions. Those emissions are not an obligation: a newly covered facility receives a baseline, and only the gap between its emissions and that baseline must be covered. Under the department’s Option 2, where new entrants start near the top of the decline curve, the first-year obligation is close to zero.

The 0.92 Mt figure measures a separate effect: baseline that stops being granted to facilities already regulated, reporting and covered. That reduction translates directly into a tighter aggregate compliance limit for existing facilities. It applies through existing reporting and compliance arrangements, but it is not included in the consultation paper’s threshold table.

Why the effect changes sharply at particular thresholds

Three qualifications affect the estimate.

The estimate is a lower bound

For the 24 covered facilities already on the minimum baseline, the published 100,000 tonnes is the floored value; the calculated baseline behind it is lower, by an amount the register does not disclose. Projecting 100,000 forward therefore overstates their unfloored FY2029–30 baseline and understates the difference between the minimum baseline and the calculated baseline.

The effect changes sharply at particular thresholds

The effect changes unevenly because 24 facilities share the same published 100,000-tonne baseline and therefore project to the same 72,838 tonnes in 2029–30. At a 75,000-tonne threshold they remain just under the new minimum and stay floored; at 50,000 tonnes they move entirely off the floor. The precise transition depends on calculated baseline values that are not publicly disclosed. The distribution of projected baselines is charted in the appendix.

The analysis does not identify a preferred threshold

This analysis does not assess which coverage threshold should be adopted. It identifies an effect that should be included when the options are compared. The department’s reasoning against a 25,000-tonne threshold (291 new facilities, 18 new industry groups, administrative cost outrunning abatement) is not examined here.

Which sectors are most exposed

The practical effect is concentrated in resource sectors.

Both the department and the Authority raise the same concern. Net emissions fell 7.4 per cent and then 5.5 per cent across the two post-reform years, while gross emissions, before credits, fell 1.9 and 2.4 per cent. The difference was met with credits. The Authority states that material onsite abatement “appears limited to a few facilities”.

The facility register shows that credit use is concentrated in resource sectors.

Four-fifths of offset-credit surrender comes from three resource sectors: coal mining 48 per cent, oil and gas extraction 18 per cent and metal ore mining 15 per cent of the 10.5 million credits surrendered in FY2024–25. All non-resource sectors combined account for 19 per cent. The sector chart and totals, in the appendix →

Of the 24 facilities that surrendered offset credits equal to 30 per cent or more of their baseline — the threshold at which the regulator requires a written explanation of why more was not done on site — 22 are coal or metal ore mines: 12 and 10 respectively.

The measures under consideration, including quantitative limits on credit use, discounting so that more than one credit is needed per tonne, and vintage restrictions, would apply across the scheme. Their immediate effect would fall disproportionately on mining facilities. All three assume that the party holding the baseline can respond by investing on site. For a large and identifiable class of mines, it cannot.

A separate proposal raises an ownership problem at mine power stations

A lower threshold is only one proposal in the review. A separate proposal, tighter limits on ACCU use, raises an ownership problem for contractor-operated mine power stations.

At an off-grid mine, onsite abatement means changing what the power station burns or adding solar and storage in front of it. Heliovulcan examined station ownership at 39 Australian gold operations with onsite generation: 35 stations were contractor-owned, two were miner-owned and two were unresolved. Fifteen operations disclosed battery storage, and in each identified case the battery sat inside the power provider’s asset and contract.

The mine pays the Safeguard cost, but the power contractor may control the equipment needed to reduce emissions. Unless the contract passes that cost or benefit to the contractor, a higher credit price may not lead to investment. Before strengthening the price signal, the review should consider whether the party carrying the obligation also controls the relevant abatement asset. For this class of facility that is knowable only site by site.

The department projects the largest increase in facility numbers under a lower threshold in metal ore mining and oil and gas extraction. Many of those facilities are remote, off-grid or weak-grid operations with onsite power systems. The site-by-site evidence, the responsibility map and the coal comparison are in the technical appendix.

Implications for the threshold review

  1. Include the baseline-floor effect in the options table. The consultation currently quantifies newly covered emissions but not the reduction in baselines for existing facilities.
  2. Separate coverage from compliance impact. Emissions entering the scheme are not the same as the immediate surrender obligation created by the change.
  3. Test whether the regulated party controls the abatement asset. At contractor-operated mine power stations, the facility carrying the Safeguard obligation may not control investment in generation, fuel switching or storage.

A threshold decision should therefore be assessed against both populations: facilities entering the scheme and facilities already covered whose minimum baseline would fall.

The three review documents

Three review processes address related parts of the scheme, and each hands work to the next.

The Carbon Leakage Review, led by Frank Jotzo and published in February, closed with a recommendation to introduce a border carbon adjustment for cement and clinker first, to consider lime, hydrogen and ammonia, steel and iron, and glass later, to refuse export rebates outright, and — the part that matters here — to remove concessional trade-exposed baselines for any commodity once a border adjustment covers it. Its recommendations were handed to the Safeguard review.

The Climate Change Authority was given a deliberately narrow brief: the post-2030 baseline decline rate, and the extent to which the reforms are actually driving abatement at the facilities themselves. Its preliminary arithmetic puts a decline rate of about 4.5 per cent a year against the bottom of Australia’s 2035 target range and about 7 per cent against the top — against an indicative 3.285 per cent currently written into the rules. It says explicitly that the coverage threshold and international units are outside its scope.

The department’s consultation paper is the wide one. It carries the decline rate, credit restrictions, trade-exposed concessions, the border adjustment, banking and borrowing, coal baselines, road-versus-rail coverage, and the coverage threshold. It opens by stating that the review “is not intended to make fundamental reforms” and that changes will generally take effect from 1 July 2030.

Within that, the coverage threshold gets a short section and a clean table: drop from 100,000 tonnes to 75,000 and 48 more facilities come in, carrying 4.1 Mt of newly covered emissions; drop to 50,000 and it is 108 facilities and 7.8 Mt. The biggest increases, the paper notes, are in metal ore mining and oil and gas extraction.

How this was done

How we rebuilt the baseline calculation and checked it

The mine population outside the register

Four hundred and thirty-three Australian mines sit outside the Safeguard register today. Two hundred and forty-five of them are operating. Of those, 98 are off-grid — 49 gold and silver operations, 19 iron ore, the balance spread across mineral sands, base metals and bauxite. A further 54 sit on a weak connection.

Whether any individual one crosses a 75,000 or 50,000-tonne threshold depends on emissions we cannot compute from outside, and this analysis does not claim to identify them. What it does establish is the character of the population: a lower threshold does not mainly admit factories. It admits remote sites that make their own electricity, where the abatement lever is a physical power station with a fuel bill and a contract, and where — on the gold evidence above — the odds are that the station belongs to a third party.

A threshold change converts a discretionary cost decision at an off-grid power station into a regulated one. It does not change the physics or the capital cost. It changes who has to have the conversation, and when.

Station ownership at mine power stations

This evidence supports the ownership point made in the main article. Heliovulcan built it site by site; none of the three facts below appears in any national register.

At an off-grid mine, onsite abatement means changing what the power station burns or adding solar and storage in front of it. That is the only lever of any scale, and at most sites the mine does not hold it.

We have gone through the Australian gold sector site by site — 88 operations carrying a Safeguard or pollutant-inventory identity, with station ownership, storage and contract expiry established from contractor project registers, state generation licences and company filings.

Station ownership was examined for 39 gold operations with onsite generation. Thirty-five stations were contractor-owned, typically by one of about seven fund-owned independent power producers under a build-own-operate contract with a term of a decade or more; two were miner-owned and two remained unresolved.

  • Storage sits inside the contractor’s asset. Fifteen of the operations disclosed battery storage; in each identified case, the battery was installed within the power provider’s asset and contract structure.
  • The obligation and the asset sit with different parties. The mine reports the generation emissions and holds the baseline. The contractor may own the plant, choose the fuel and receive the fuel saving.
  • Contract expiry is rarely public. A contract expiry date was identified in public sources for nine of the 88 operations. No public expiry date was found for the remainder.

Coal baselines and the generation-side market

The same caution applies, for a different reason, to coal. Both papers raise ending the concession that lets existing coal mines keep half of their baseline on facility-specific intensity past 2030 while everyone else converges to the industry average. Doing so would raise the liability on gassy mines — correctly, on the scheme’s own logic. But of 102 Australian coal mines in our screen, none is off-grid, and the emissions at issue are ventilation air methane and haul fleet diesel. Tightening coal baselines creates a compliance cost. It does not create a generation-side market, and any business case built on the assumption that it does will be wrong by an order of magnitude.

Three disciplines for reading a coverage proposal, in full

Three points follow from this analysis. Read the mechanism, not only the summary table. The largest quantified consequence in the coverage section of a 48-page consultation paper is one sentence about the minimum baseline, and it is visible only after the calculation is rebuilt. Ask who holds the abatement asset before asking whether the price signal is strong enough. Credit discounting applies to the party holding the baseline, and at most off-grid mines the generating asset belongs to a contractor on a decade-long contract. Separate the compliance cost from the addressable market. Tightening coal baselines creates a real compliance cost but little generation-side opportunity, because the emissions are ventilation methane and haul-fleet diesel rather than a power station. A site with little reported electricity can still become a large electrical load

Market screens for mine energy usually start from what a site consumes today: purchased electricity, or the capacity of the power station on site. A diesel-powered mine records most of its energy as fuel, not electricity. Its haul trucks, dozers, drills and light vehicles appear in the national accounts as mining diesel; they appear in the electricity data hardly at all. A screen based on current electrical load therefore ranks such a site as small, when its future load could be among the largest in the sector.

One screened site shows the effect. The electricity datasets reviewed record neither purchased electricity nor reported generation for one remote gold operation, while the project’s environmental material shows that the site is supplied by diesel plant. Converting its reported fuel use gives an average electrical equivalent of 8.7–10.4 MW. Applying the average-to-installed ratio typical of the off-grid mine stations in Heliovulcan’s screen, roughly three to one, implies approximately 25–35 MW of installed capacity; this is an indicative range, not a verified station specification, and several times the size of the power station at a neighbouring operation in the same group. A layer built on who has a power station today classifies that site as nothing at all.

Current electricity-demand data do not include fuel used by mobile mining equipment, so a screen based only on generation or purchased electricity will not identify these sites.

Fuel tax credits raise the economic threshold for electrification

Off-road mining diesel is eligible for the full fuel tax credit, so the private cost of each litre is lower than its excise-inclusive price, and every litre displaced forgoes the credit. Electrification therefore has to compete against the net-of-credit diesel cost. At the full off-road rate, the reported mining diesel pool is associated with approximately A$3.9 billion a year of fuel tax credits (metal ore and quarrying about A$1.9 billion, coal mining about A$2.0 billion). This is a national-order exposure, not the cost of an achievable electrification programme: only fuel actually displaced would forgo the credit. Any mine energy business case has to be net of it.

Fleet replacement and mine life will help determine which projects can move first

Haul-truck service life and remaining mine life are often of the same order. A mine with insufficient remaining life to recover the fleet and infrastructure investment is unlikely to electrify without a transferable asset, a leasing structure or a residual-value solution. A site is a candidate when it meets all of the following:

  • large mobile diesel exposure;
  • fleet replacement approaching;
  • sufficient remaining mine life to amortise the fleet and the power system;
  • grid or onsite generation that can be expanded;
  • a charging duty cycle that the operation can accommodate;
  • a power contract that can be adjusted before its term;
  • owner and operator interests that align on who funds the upgrade.

Fleet age is not publicly available site by site. Mine life and contract expiry are. That asymmetry should decide the order in which a screen asks its questions.

Implications for market screening

  1. Do not rank mines only by current electricity consumption.
  2. Estimate mobile diesel exposure separately from stationary generation.
  3. Treat the theoretical electrical equivalent as a ceiling on energy, not a market.
  4. Check fleet replacement and mine life before modelling infrastructure.
  5. Model fuel tax credits and charging capacity explicitly.
  6. Identify who owns the fleet and who must fund the power-system upgrade.

Current electricity data will miss many future mine-electrification loads. Market estimates should start with diesel use, then screen each site for fleet timing, mine life, power supply and tax treatment. Turning the theoretical electrical equivalent into an addressable market requires facility-level evidence on all of those.

Australia has public funding programs for industrial decarbonisation, and mine sites are eligible for several of them. On our register of 23 mine microgrid and battery projects, we found publicly disclosed funding for three, each of which reached commercial operation by 2020. That pattern is worth understanding before building a funding case.

Grant announcements do not show who can use the funding

A grant is the expected answer to how a remote mine funds a hybrid energy project, and for recent projects it is usually the wrong one. The projects built in recent years, including Tropicana’s 115 MW hybrid, Kathleen Valley, Bellevue and Rio Tinto’s Pilbara renewables program, were funded commercially: either on an independent power producer’s balance sheet against a power purchase agreement, or as miner capital expenditure.

Public funding serves a specific purpose. Applying to the wrong program can cost months of development time. Which program applies to a given site, and whether any applies, belongs in the early screen rather than in later financing work.

What the funding record shows

Each project on our register with publicly disclosed funding was described as a first application of its kind:

  • Weipa (2015) — the first remote Australian mining PV installation with no grid access.
  • DeGrussa (2016) — the first at meaningful scale, combining grant support with concessional debt.
  • Agnew (2020) — the first large-scale wind generation at an Australian mine.

We found no public funding record for the projects that followed. The small sample suggests that a funding case is stronger when the project can show a first application, a replicable lesson or a risk that private finance will not yet carry.

The useful question is which part of the project is still genuinely unproven.

That reframing usually moves the conversation off the base system and onto the specific thing a given site is actually pioneering: fully inverter-based islanding at scale, long-duration storage, haul fleet electrification, or replacing process heat with electricity. Those are where the risk that public funding exists to absorb still lives.

Eligibility can be tested from facility attributes already held

The useful discipline here is to stop reading program pages as prose and start reading them as tests against facts you already have on the site. Most Australian program eligibility decomposes into a handful of attributes:

  • Jurisdiction — and, for several federal programs, whether the facility sits outside a greater capital city area.
  • Sector — some programs are drawn tightly around one commodity.
  • Reporting status — whether the facility reports under the National Greenhouse and Energy Reporting Act.
  • Safeguard coverage — and, for one stream, whether the facility is trade-exposed.
  • Emissions threshold — which cuts both ways: at least one state program is aimed below 100,000 tonnes a year, not above it.
  • Applicant entity — who has to hold the application. See below; this is the one that catches people.

Assembled that way, the map answers a site-specific question in the screening phase, when the answer can still change the design.

As a snapshot of the current shape — as at 3 August 2026, and to be verified before it is relied on, because rounds open and close continually:

  • At federal level, the Powering the Regions Fund runs two relevant streams: an industrial transformation stream administered by ARENA, whose eligibility turns on being an NGER-reporting facility outside a capital city, and a safeguard transformation stream for trade-exposed Safeguard facilities, which co-funds up to half of eligible expenditure and runs in defined application batches.
  • In Queensland, the Low Emissions Investment Partnerships program is a $520 million commitment funded from coal royalties. It is not a competitive grant round — partnerships are negotiated bilaterally — and its stated focus is metallurgical coal mines covered by the Safeguard Mechanism, including diesel displacement in mining fleets and ventilation air methane destruction.
  • In Western Australia, the Clean Energy Future Fund is comparatively small and co-funds a smaller share, but its stated priorities have included off-grid electricity supply, long-duration storage and heavy industry decarbonisation, and it has backed mine-relevant work including haul truck conversion and diesel-to-battery replacement. Note that the much larger state clean energy fund announced in 2026 is directed at transmission, not at off-grid sites.
  • In New South Wales, industrial decarbonisation funding has been split between a program for facilities below the 100,000 tonne threshold and a separate high-emitting industries program, with rounds opening and closing rather than running continuously.
  • For large capital programs in northern Australia, concessional debt is usually the more consequential instrument than any grant. A few million dollars of co-funding does not change a decision on a several-hundred-million-dollar program; the cost and tenor of debt does.

Who holds the application depends on who owns the power station

This is the part that most often turns a promising funding route into a dead end late in the process.

Across the mine energy projects we track, the overwhelming majority of power stations are owned and operated by an independent power producer — the miner contracts for power and does not own the generating asset. In at least one large operation the miner holds no equity in the power station at all while carrying the entirety of its reported emissions.

Meanwhile, the eligibility language on the main federal streams points at the party with operational control of the reporting facility. That is the miner.

The money can generally only be applied for by the party that does not own the asset the money would build.

This is resolvable — through the miner funding a defined scope, through a joint application, or through the miner taking ownership of a specific element — but it has to be resolved deliberately, and it has consequences for who carries the asset, who takes the residual value risk, and how the power purchase agreement is priced. It has to be settled alongside the technical case. It is part of the technical case.

The benefit has to be stated net of fuel tax credits

One more discipline, and it is the one most likely to change a headline figure. A remote mine running on diesel is already receiving a substantial government payment in the form of fuel tax credits. On a large operation that credit can be an order of magnitude larger than the site’s entire carbon compliance cost.

Displace the diesel and you displace the credit with it. The saving per litre is therefore materially lower than the pump price implies — and a business case built on the gross figure will overstate the benefit, sometimes by a quarter or more. The same logic runs in reverse at gas-fired sites, where the fuel was never attracting the credit in the first place, so displacing it has no such offset. Two sites that look similar can require opposite arithmetic.

A funding case that omits this describes a better version of the project than the one that exists.

Screen applicant eligibility before modelling project economics

Public funding belongs in financeability readiness (F4) as a set of tests against facts the screen already produces, and the answers change the design. Four questions belong in the early screen: is this facility eligible on attributes we already hold; is any part of it still unproven enough to be worth funding; who holds the application given who owns the power station; and what is the benefit net of fuel tax credits. A program with no next date attached has no place in a decision. A flat grid-price assumption is increasingly wrong

One assumption does more damage in early-stage remote-energy models than any other: that the delivered cost of grid power stays roughly flat. It is rarely challenged and increasingly wrong. For the mines, remote communities and weak-grid industrial sites Heliovulcan screens, the trajectory of delivered energy cost is often more decisive than the headline wholesale price everyone quotes.

Electricity market reform is moving towards network prices that reflect more of the cost of serving each location. A remote site is on the expensive side of that change.

The price difference that changes the project case

Britain spent about £800 million in 2021 paying wind farms to switch off and gas plants to switch on in their place. Total congestion cost there has risen roughly twenty-fold in twenty years, paid by consumers. Markets with substantial renewable generation and limited transmission face the same pressure, including Australia.

What locational pricing is

Most markets were designed around a handful of big, dispatchable stations turned up and down to follow demand. A single national price, or in the NEM a single regional price, quotes one wholesale number as if power could move from a remote wind farm to a distant factory at no cost. The cost of moving it, and of not being able to move it, is shared across every consumer’s bill instead of being charged where it arises.

Locational pricing makes that geography visible, in two strengths:

  • Zonal pricing splits a country into a handful of price zones (Norway, Sweden, Italy). This is what Britain is debating.
  • Nodal pricing / LMP prices electricity at every node — hundreds or thousands of prices (ERCOT and most US markets). The price at each point reflects exactly what it costs to deliver one more unit of power there, including losses and congestion.

The principle is identical at both strengths: where the grid is constrained, power is worth less to inject and costs more to consume.

Two signals, and which one reaches a developer

The debate involves two signals. The operational signal tells assets when to run. The investment signal tells investors where to build a data centre, electrolyser, factory or battery. In a locational market the investment signal is a visible price that a project can hedge, as investors do in ERCOT, Norway and Sweden. In a national or regional market the signal arrives through network charges instead, which are harder to predict and cannot be hedged.

The choice is between a locational price a project can hedge and a network charge it cannot — and both are aimed at constrained, remote parts of the grid.

Utility-scale battery finance is oversubscribed

Capital follows demonstrable project readiness

A delay changes both the cost and the revenue assumptions

The two dates that most often gate financial close are the connection date and, on industrial sites, the approval date. Neither should be treated as fixed: each week of delay changes the cost and revenue assumptions that follow from it.

The equipment supplier quotes against an assumed delivery date, and the customer prices its contract against the same date. If the date slips, both are reopened. The supplier adds an uncertainty premium, which raises capital cost, and the customer discounts for the same uncertainty, which lowers contracted revenue. One date therefore moves both the cost line and the revenue line:

A delay can increase capital cost, postpone or reduce revenue, and weaken debt-service coverage at the same time.

If the model holds the approval or connection date fixed, it understates both the possible cost increase and the loss or delay of revenue. Both effects can reduce the debt-service coverage ratio, the measure lenders use to test whether cash flow covers debt payments. The approvals and connection screens exist to surface that early.

A project is rarely fully contracted or fully merchant, and how much revenue is contracted sets the capital structure. A fully merchant project attracts more conservative terms: lower gearing, higher pricing, cash sweeps and a shorter term. A contracted base gives the lender revenue it can rely on for minimum debt service, which makes merchant income above it easier to fund. Some structures add a gearing step-up, where lenders agree in advance to release further debt as the sponsor secures more contracted revenue.

For an industrial energy-as-a-service structure, the contracted revenue is a fixed service fee that can be invoiced and underwritten. Merchant or behind-the-meter income can improve the equity case, but debt should not be sized against it, for the same reason that customer savings should be separated from project revenue.

A project must survive every gate. Nominal queue capacity says little about how much capacity will reach construction.

When a market reports tens of gigawatts of BESS connection applications, the easy conclusion is that supply is about to overwhelm demand. Developers see something different. Much of the queue still lacks controlled land, a buildable cable route, an acceptable planning pathway, a frozen technical solution or committed capital.

A connection queue is a portfolio of development options that have not yet survived risk selection. A project should enter the credible pipeline only after site control, planning, connection progress, delivery structure and finance have been established.

Development is a chain of survival gates

A battery project must move through site selection, land control, cable access, connection studies, environmental and planning approval, community acceptance, technical freeze, financing, procurement, construction and registration. These are not independent tasks with equal weights.

A project with 80% of its documents complete may hold far less than 80% of its value. If the final 20% contains an unobtainable easement or an unaffordable network upgrade, its completion probability may still be close to zero.

  • Land gate: Are the site, access road, construction areas and complete cable route controlled?
  • Approval gate: Does the consent pathway match the final layout, noise, fire and disturbance assumptions?
  • Grid gate: Can the connection capacity, date and technical envelope actually be relied upon?
  • Delivery gate: Do transformer, switchgear, PCS and EPC lead times align with the target COD?
  • Finance gate: Does the downside case still support capital after delay and repricing?

A planning delay can change several financial assumptions at once

Planning is often treated as a line item in the development budget. It is better understood as a risk that moves several model inputs at once. A delayed approval can shift the connection date, trigger equipment and EPC repricing, misalign an offtake or tolling start date, increase interest during construction and force the financing case to be reopened.

Community engagement belongs in the same commercial logic. Clear fire, noise, traffic and land-use evidence can reduce the risk of a lengthy delay where opposition is likely. It is part of schedule certainty.

How to read a BESS pipeline

A decision-useful pipeline should replace a single “percentage complete” with evidence for each survival gate. At minimum, it should record the right being relied upon, the document proving it, the responsible party, the latest decision date and the exit condition if the gap cannot be closed.

What the early screen checks: the approval pathway, control of the site and the cable route, and the effect of delay on cost, commissioning date and financing.   4. Delivery realism: approvals, grid, procurement, finance and construction sit on one integrated critical path.

← All research Discuss a question

A behind-the-meter battery can save its host money from the first month. It becomes financeable only when the contract turns part of those savings into a payment to the project company. A lender then discounts that payment under its downside case. This article follows those three steps, then covers the demand-charge stream, the other income streams and the questions to test before a system is quoted.

1 · Customer avoided cost

The customer saving is usually the largest and easiest figure to model in an early-stage industrial solar and battery case, and it is usually real. They should not be treated as project revenue unless the contract converts them into payments to the project company. A remote site burning diesel really does save a great deal of money by displacing fuel. The mistake is to assume that number belongs to the project company.

Early models often start with customer data such as fuel use, tariffs and load, because that is what is available first. That shows the customer’s potential saving, not the revenue available to repay project debt. A lender asks which contracted payments are available for debt service. The project may still create value, but the model does not yet show how that value will repay debt.

A solar + BESS project displaces diesel, lowers generator O&M, improves reliability and creates carbon benefit for the site owner. Together these are the customer’s avoided costs: the full value the project creates for the energy user. That value belongs to the customer unless the contract transfers part of it.

The project company receives only what the contract says it receives. A lender then sizes debt against the portion of that revenue that is contracted, durable and protected under its downside case. Three different amounts are involved: what the customer saves, what the project company is paid, and what a lender will lend against.

Customer value is necessary, but only contracted revenue supports debt service, and only the part that survives the lender’s downside case.

3 · Lender-recognised cash flow

The analysis separates project revenue into three cases, each answering a different party’s question:

S1 · contracted base

Fixed, contracted EaaS fee only

The predictable payment obligation a lender can actually underwrite. This is the foundation. If S1 does not clear the DSCR threshold, the project is not financeable under the current structure — however good the customer economics look.

S2 · customer upside

Fee plus behind-the-meter and customer-side value

Additional value — arbitrage, spare-capacity use, the customer’s wider avoided cost. It strengthens the equity case and the customer’s willingness to sign. But it depends on dispatch rights and behaviour, so a careful lender discounts or excludes it.

S3 · lender downside

The stressed, bankable case

S1 run through the lender’s pessimism: lower availability, customer under-use, delay, credit stress. This is the case debt is actually sized against. If the project fails under the stressed contracted-revenue case, the model does not yet support the proposed debt structure.

S1 determines debt capacity, S2 adds potential equity value, and S3 tests whether debt remains supportable under downside assumptions.

An explicit but unfavourable network charge can be modelled, haircut and priced. A material charging rule that remains undefined after 2029 is harder: it may prevent lenders from forming a credible downside case. The same logic applies to connection constraints, marginal loss factors and market redesign.

A generic ±10% regulatory sensitivity is not enough when a rule change can alter whether the asset may participate, when it can energise or which network charges it must carry.

Arbitrage, FCAS, capacity and balancing are often presented as separate columns. In operation, the battery buys and sells across markets while constrained by the same state of charge, availability, efficiency, degradation and connection capacity. Charging may appear as negative revenue in one market before enabling a profitable discharge elsewhere.

A financeable model therefore needs a single physical ledger:

  • interval charge and discharge volumes and prices;
  • state of charge, efficiency, availability and degradation;
  • net settlement across all markets;
  • optimiser fees, imbalance and redispatch outcomes;
  • conflicts where the same capacity cannot perform two services simultaneously.

Compare these streams by the net revenue each adds per cycle and per MW, after the cost of the cycling they require.

4 · Demand-charge reduction, as one example

For customers on demand-based network tariffs, peak-demand reduction is the value stream that can be attributed most directly to a battery, and the one most easily converted into a contracted fee. A commercial bill combines an energy charge, fixed charges and a demand charge. The energy component measures how much electricity the site uses; the demand component measures how fast it uses electricity at its highest point.

Two facilities can use very different amounts of energy and still face the same demand charge if they reach the same peak. A compressor, chiller, crusher or group of fast chargers can set that peak in a single interval. A battery behind the main meter discharges to hold a target demand ceiling, then recharges when load and tariff conditions allow. It does not need to reduce total consumption.

The control problem is harder than it sounds. The system must decide how much charge to keep in reserve and which peak is likely to matter, because one missed peak can reset the billing maximum and remove most of the month’s expected saving. Australian commercial and industrial tariffs recover part of network cost through measured peak kW or kVA, sometimes within defined time windows and sometimes with ratchet effects, so the opportunity is specific to the site and the tariff. The first task is therefore not choosing a battery. It is reconstructing the delivered cost from bills, interval data and the applicable network tariff.

5 · Other value streams

The same battery may support several value streams:

  • Peak-demand shaving against a kW or kVA network charge.
  • Tariff optimisation, including staying below a threshold that changes the applicable tariff.
  • Energy arbitrage between low- and high-price periods.
  • Demand response, FCAS or network support, where market access and operating constraints permit.
  • Solar self-consumption by shifting surplus generation into a later load period.

Each benefit in a stack has to be deliverable at the same time as the others. Each service competes for power, energy capacity and state of charge. The defensible case is the compatible stack the control system can actually deliver under the customer’s tariff and operating profile.

Harmony Energy’s experience with two-hour batteries provides a useful way to think about duration. A shorter-duration asset may appear superior while high-value ancillary services are deep and unsaturated. If those services compress earlier than expected, the asset can lose both revenue and strategic flexibility.

Additional duration may lower the central-case IRR while expanding the set of markets the battery can enter and reducing dependence on one service. The investment decision should therefore compare more than the expected return. It should test capital survival under service saturation, spread compression and dispatch error.

Battery duration changes more than the MWh figure. It can be an insurance premium against getting the future market structure wrong.

For customers under emissions reporting, safeguard obligations or internal decarbonisation targets, diesel displacement carries genuine carbon value. It should be quantified and shown — it is often why the customer is at the table. But it needs discipline: the carbon benefit belongs to the customer unless the contract explicitly assigns it to the SPV, and a lender will not underwrite carbon revenue unless it is contracted, certified and stable, which it rarely is early on. Counting it in both places would double-count the benefit.

The better question is who owns the carbon benefit, and whether it is contracted.

6 · Stress case

Failure to meet the lender case may indicate a problem with revenue allocation, contract tenor, credit support or gearing rather than with the underlying technology. The problem may lie in the contract or the financing structure rather than in the battery. The changes that usually matter are:

  • a higher fixed fee, so the project captures more of the customer saving;
  • a longer contract term;
  • a take-or-pay floor under the payment;
  • stronger customer credit or termination provisions;
  • lower gearing;
  • a different repayment profile, where tail-year sculpting has dropped the minimum coverage ratio.

The appropriate conclusion is that the financing structure needs work, not that the project is unviable.

  • Size to the shape and volatility of the load. Nameplate peak alone does not reveal how long the event lasts or how predictable it is.
  • Derate forecast savings. Volatile loads, solar variability, outages and changing operating schedules reduce perfect-model performance.
  • Check tariff rules. Billing windows, kVA measurement, ratchets and threshold changes can matter more than the nominal demand rate.
  • Separate resilience. A battery sized to clip short peaks is not automatically capable of carrying critical load through an outage.
  • Test degradation and availability. The valuable peak may occur when the battery is unavailable, energy-limited or preserving warranty constraints.
  1. Debt foundation: repeatable or contracted revenue with transparent lender haircuts.
  2. Operating scenarios: service saturation, spread compression, availability, degradation, delay and optimiser underperformance.
  3. Equity optionality: upside from volatility, new services and rule changes without relying on it to support DSCR.

COD delay should also flow through equipment repricing, contract commencement, interest during construction and the revenue curve. Simply shifting revenue one year to the right understates the damage.

The customer does not always need to fund the asset directly. Shared-savings agreements, fixed service payments, solar-plus-storage PPAs and performance contracts can convert an uncertain capital decision into an operating-cost proposition. But the contract must say who carries performance risk when the peak is missed, the tariff changes or the operating profile moves.

7 · Questions to test

Before a system is quoted, the following can be answered from the customer’s interval data, tariff and draft contract. If any answer is missing, the model is describing a value pool, not a financeable project.

Questions to ask

  • Which party owns each saving and environmental benefit?
  • What payment is fixed, contracted and enforceable?
  • How long does the contract run relative to the debt and asset life?
  • What remains after availability, operating cost and lender haircuts?
  • Does the stressed CFADS still cover debt service?
  • For a demand tariff, which interval sets the charge? How predictable is the peak, and how much can the proposed battery reduce on the worst day?
  • Which other services can the same battery deliver at the same time as peak defence, under the customer’s operating profile?

Method

A Pre-DD screen is not a feasibility study

Method 19 June 2026 5-minute read Research note

← All research Discuss a question

A feasibility study develops and tests a selected project design. A Pre-DD screen comes earlier: it identifies approval, site, connection and financeability issues before significant development cost is committed, while the answer is still cheap to act on.

At a glance
  1. The useful early-stage output is an evidence-based decision: proceed, redesign, reduce scope, pause or stop.
  2. An industrial solar and battery project carries approval, land, connection, contract and cash flow risk, with an energy model inside it. An attractive modelled IRR is not meaningful if the model ignores land, approvals, connection or contract risk.
  3. The four screens cover four common reasons a project cannot proceed: approvals, site suitability, the microgrid or connection boundary, and the financing case.
  4. Inside those sit five working questions: technical fit, customer avoided cost, contract revenue, carbon value and bankability. Problems often arise where technical, commercial and contractual responsibilities meet.
  5. A good screen does not remove uncertainty. It makes uncertainty visible early enough that the project can still be reshaped cheaply.

An early project discussion may move quickly to equipment size and projected returns. That can be premature if land, approvals, connection and contracts have not been checked. If those issues are found later, the developer may have spent money testing a design that cannot proceed.

A Pre-DD screen is Heliovulcan’s early-stage screen, not an industry-standard term. It is deliberately cheaper and more sceptical, and its job is to find, from public information, the most likely reasons this project could not proceed, and whether they can still be designed around. That is a different instrument for a different moment.

The useful output is a decision

Early-stage data is soft by nature: load is approximate, diesel prices move, EPC costs are indicative, customer credit is unverified, debt terms are unnegotiated. A screen that reports a precise IRR to three decimal places gives false confidence. The appropriate output is a decision the project owner can act on:

Proceed as-is, redesign the structure, reduce the scope, pause for missing data, or stop.

Each of those is a good outcome if it is reached early. Stopping after a public-data screen costs a few weeks. Stopping after contractor quotation, legal structuring and investor engagement costs a great deal more.

Four questions come before the energy model

A spreadsheet can make almost any project look attractive if it assumes the hard parts away. Those parts are rarely the energy balance:

  • Approval — does the project fit the existing pathway, or trigger new disturbance, licence, water, heritage or closure issues?
  • Land and site — is there somewhere the equipment can physically and legally sit?
  • Load and connection — what slice of the load can actually be served, and where is the microgrid boundary?
  • Contract — can the value be converted into a payment obligation someone is bound to pay?
  • Financeability — does the resulting cash flow support debt on terms a lender will accept?

F1–F4: a practical screen

The four screens map onto exactly those break points. They are run together, not in strict sequence — but each asks one clear question.

F1 · Approval Delta

What new approvals, variations or conditions does adding PV+BESS trigger, relative to the site’s existing consents? The delta is what adds time and risk.

F2 · Site Suitability

Where can the array, storage, PCS and interconnection realistically go, given layout, setbacks, watercourses, roads, pipelines and closure areas visible on public maps? “Empty land” on satellite is not the same as usable, consentable land.

F3 · Microgrid / Connection Boundary

What load slice can actually be served, and how do PV, BESS, diesel, PCS, EMS and site loads connect? This sets the boundary the commercial case has to live inside — explored in Weak-grid mines buy reliability as well as energy.

F4 · Financeability Readiness

Which cash flow can be contracted, invoiced and underwritten? This is where customer savings and project revenue get separated — the subject of the first mistake in BTM BESS feasibility.

The five questions inside the screen

Underneath F1–F4 sit five working questions I keep returning to. They interact, and most projects fail at a boundary between two of them:

  • Technical fit — is the system sized to the real, predictable load?
  • Customer avoided cost — what does the customer genuinely stop paying, against a credible baseline?
  • Contract revenue — how much of that avoided cost becomes stable, contracted SPV revenue?
  • Carbon value — is it quantified, correctly assigned, and kept out of double-counting?
  • Bankability — does the contracted revenue support debt service at a coverage ratio a lender will accept?

Notice that four of the five are commercial and financial, not technical. Whether the battery works is rarely the binding question. Whether the project can be paid for, contracted and financed, and for whom it creates value, determines its viability.

The model should not only ask: what is the IRR? It should ask: what contract produces that IRR, and would anyone lend against it?

The question a screen answers

An early screen answers one question: on the public evidence, should this project proceed as proposed, be redesigned, be reduced in scope, wait for missing data, or stop? It gives that answer while the cost of changing direction is still small.

Project insight

Screening energy options for weak-grid and off-grid mines

Project insight 26 June 2026 7-minute read Research note

← All research Discuss a question

A weak-grid or off-grid mine buys reliability as well as energy. The served load is the part of site demand the proposed system is expected to supply, and the sites where a hybrid system makes the most sense are often the ones a lowest-cost or current-load screen misreads. This note sets out the seven questions to settle before a system is designed: the load that can be served reliably, how quickly power is needed, what diesel is doing today, what the connection route risks, whether mine life supports the tenor, where the commercial boundary sits, and when a feasibility study is worth commissioning.

In this note 1 · The load and the reliability the site requires 2 · Supply options and connection risk 3 · Mine life and contract term 4 · The commercial boundary, and when to commission a feasibility study

1 · The load and the reliability the site requires

Screening a mine-site project like a commercial rooftop, as annual energy times a tariff less the cost of the system, produces a misleading number. A commercial building can often assess electricity primarily as an operating cost. At a remote mine, power reliability is also a production constraint, so the cost of interruption has to be included in the energy decision. When the plant stops, the loss is measured in lost production per hour, not cents per kWh. That single difference reorders the whole problem.

A common early-stage error is to design for the maximum renewable share while assuming away the operating role diesel still performs. A screen that respects operational continuity looks different from the first line.

Mines and heavy industrial sites often run peak loads several times their average, driven by intermittent high-draw equipment. Sizing a solar and battery system to meet those rare peaks raises capital cost while utilisation falls, and the economics fail before a contract is drafted. The better approach is to size for the sustained, predictable load: average demand, night load and baseload consumption. Diesel keeps the peaks. The BESS is asked to displace a meaningful, contractable share of diesel energy, and to do it every day.

The screen therefore turns on one question:

Which part of site demand can be served repeatedly, reliably and under contract?

What diesel is doing today

Diesel generation at a remote mine performs several jobs at once. A model that treats diesel only as an energy source will underestimate the capacity and reliability the replacement system needs:

  • Energy supply — the baseload kilowatt-hours the site runs on.
  • Capacity reserve — firm capacity available on demand, regardless of weather.
  • Backup — cover for solar variability and equipment outages.
  • Peak support — the buffer for mill start-ups, hoist cycles and compressor surges.
  • Contingency supply — power during rare or extended shortfalls.

Only the first of those is straightforward to displace with PV and storage. The others are why the site keeps diesel on even when it is expensive. A design that assumes diesel can simply be removed misreads what the customer is buying. The practical question is which part of the load can be served reliably and economically by solar and storage, while diesel stays available for peaks and contingencies.

The operating architecture that follows coordinates energy, fast response and firm capacity around the production load, with a defined control boundary:

Solar PVLow-cost daytime energy
BESSShifting, ramps, peaks, reserve
Diesel / gasDeep firming and contingency
Microgrid controllerDispatch, protection and stability
Mine loadProduction and critical services
Grid / future optionImport, export or later connection

Reliability is a binding constraint. The controller must make the portfolio behave as one power station.

The Paces off-grid analysis reports that high renewable shares can remain competitive when firming generation is retained, while the final move towards 100% renewable supply becomes disproportionately expensive. The system must then overbuild generation and storage to cover rare low-resource periods.

For a mine or industrial site, the practical architecture is therefore not “battery instead of engines”. It is:

  • a renewable block carrying a large share of annual energy;
  • BESS providing shifting, ramp control, peak support and short-duration firming;
  • diesel or gas generation retained for deep lulls, contingencies and reliability;
  • a future grid connection, longer-duration asset or additional renewable block treated as an option rather than a prerequisite.

The final increment towards 100% renewable supply can require disproportionate generation, storage and backup capacity. The preferred share is therefore a reliability and capital decision, not only an energy-cost decision.

2 · Supply options and connection risk

Two questions decide the supply route: how quickly power is needed, and what a grid connection would actually deliver.

Time to power

Paces’ starting point is a sobering development statistic: only a minority of projects taken into a utility-scale pipeline ultimately reach construction. Many projects that look viable on a spreadsheet fail later on grid cost, permitting, land or community constraints — after substantial time and money have already been committed.

The useful lesson is sequencing. Traditional development often spends deeply before the most dangerous assumptions have been tested. A better process scores completion risk early and advances only sites with a credible pathway through interconnection, approvals and procurement.

The most valuable early work is usually not making the model more precise. It is finding the reason the project may never be built.

For fast-growing loads such as data centres, a grid offer several years away may have little practical value. The opportunity cost of delay can exceed the premium for building dedicated generation beside the load. That pushes projects behind the meter and changes the comparison from which option has the lowest standalone cost of energy to which option can deliver reliable power within the required time.

Remote mines have always faced a version of the same decision. They build power because extending or strengthening the grid is slow, expensive or impossible. What is changing is that the same logic is now being validated by large, capital-rich energy users.

Connection risk

  • Queue position is not usable capacity. A project can wait behind speculative applications and still receive an uneconomic connection outcome.
  • Upgrade cost arrives late. A transformer, substation or protection requirement can destroy the economics after the site has already absorbed development spend.
  • Grid conditions change while the project waits. Available capacity can be consumed by other projects, and assumptions made at origination may not survive the study process.
  • Planning risk can move in the opposite direction. Areas with attractive grid capacity may attract concentrated development and trigger local opposition or new restrictions.

Development schedules can be lost in procurement as easily as in permitting. Long-lead transformers, switchgear, PCS, battery containers and generation equipment may decide when a project can energise. A credible time-to-power plan therefore needs supplier engagement, lead-time evidence, deposit assumptions and interface ownership early in development.

3 · Mine life and contract term

Remaining mine life sets the tenor over which any energy asset and its contract can be recovered. A hybrid system with a fifteen-year design life at a mine with six years of reserves is either a short-term contract with a residual-value problem, a redeployable asset, or a project that cannot recover its cost within the available mine or contract life. Mine life and the expiry of the existing power contract are public for most operations; fleet age and load data are not. The screen therefore checks mine life and contract term first, and asks the owner for the rest.

4 · The commercial boundary, and when to commission a feasibility study

The reliability requirement directly affects equipment size, redundancy, capital cost and the amount of debt the project can support. Which loads are critical, and how long they must keep operating during an outage, determine:

  • BESS duration and how much autonomy is really required
  • PCS sizing and whether grid-forming capability is needed
  • Redundancy (N+1) and spinning-reserve assumptions
  • O&M intensity and availability guarantees
  • CAPEX — and therefore the debt the cash flow has to carry

The required reliability standard affects both design cost and financeability. A system can meet the reliability requirement and still fail the financing test, because the added capital cost exceeds what the contracted diesel saving can support. Size the system to what the commercial structure can support, rather than to the maximum renewable share that is technically achievable.

The part of site demand that can be served reliably and under contract defines the scope used in the commercial model. The diesel saving inside that boundary has to become a contracted cash flow before it is project revenue; how that conversion works is set out in From customer savings to financeable project cash flow.

When to proceed to a feasibility study

  1. An early screen pays for itself. It identifies approval, site, grid, boundary and financing gaps before formal development spending begins.
  2. Off-grid hybrid is an economic pathway in its own right. For remote and constrained sites it can provide a faster, self-controlled route to power while preserving reliability.
  3. Schedule and equipment belong in the first client conversation. A technically attractive design without a credible procurement and energisation pathway is not development-ready.

A project moves from screen to feasibility study when the served-load boundary is defined, the connection or islanded route is chosen on time-to-power grounds, mine life and contract term support the tenor, and the owner has supplied load, diesel and reliability data. Until then, a feasibility study would be proving a design that may be answering the wrong question. How the F1–F4 project screen works

Claim types, confidence and limits.

Every figure Heliovulcan publishes carries a claim type and a confidence rating. This page defines the F1–F4 screening framework those claims come from, and — more importantly — what each claim type does and does not mean.

The F1–F4 framework Claim types Confidence scale Load reconstruction Financeability screen Siting screen What this is not

The F1–F4 framework

A Pre-DD screen asks four questions, in order, before serious development cost is committed:

  • F1 · Additional approvals required — what additional approvals would a hybrid-energy addition trigger beyond what the site already holds, and who must answer that?
  • F2 · Site suitability — do publicly visible constraints and opportunities leave credible search areas for PV and BESS/PCS near the load?
  • F3 · Microgrid boundary — where does the SPV's electrical and commercial boundary sit relative to the mine's own assets?
  • F4 · The financing case — can a third-party SPV recover its capital within the available contract life and still leave the owner better off than the diesel baseline?

The output of a screen is a recommendation to proceed, redesign, obtain more information or stop, together with a list of the data to request next — not a design, a financing decision or an investment recommendation.

Claim types

Each published claim is typed by where its content comes from:

  • Public source (public_source) — stated directly in a public document, such as a draft environmental impact statement, cited with section and page.
  • Reported (reported) — disclosed publicly by a company or third party; repeated here as reported, not independently verified.
  • Derived (derived) — obtained from public figures through a documented reconstruction or cross-check (see load reconstruction).
  • Calculated (calculated) — an output of Heliovulcan's screening model under stated assumptions; indicative only.
  • Interpreted (interpreted) — an analyst judgement, such as which scenario to validate next.
  • Assumption (assumption) — an input chosen by the analyst, such as solar yield, with its basis noted.
  • Owner confirmation required (owner_confirmation_required) — cannot be settled from public information; requires the owner, EPC contractor, lender or a specialist.
  • Unresolved (unresolved) — a known gap, left open rather than filled in.

Every published claim has an ID and links to supporting evidence, and every piece of evidence names its source. If we cannot verify a claim, we mark it unresolved rather than filling the gap. We do not invent sources, dates, page numbers or document content.

Confidence scale

  • high — stated directly in a quality public source, or a definitional statement about the screen itself;
  • medium — derived, reported or assumed with a reasonable public basis, but with known gaps (e.g. no primary URL captured, or a unit inconsistency in the source);
  • low — screening-model outputs under unverified timing, pricing, financing and contract assumptions. Useful for ranking scenarios, not for pricing transactions.

Confidence describes how well the claim is supported today, not how important it is.

Load reconstruction

Method ID METHOD-LOAD-RECONSTRUCTION-01

Annual energy and peak load are reconstructed from public environmental-impact material. In this case: the draft EIS states a proposed diesel power station of three 1.5 MW units (3.9 MW peak) burning ~7,500 kL per year of diesel, and its energy table reports 107,853 GJ per year of energy produced — equivalent to about 30 GWh per year. The EIS text itself says “approximately 30 MWh per year”, an apparent unit error; the registry records the derived about 30 GWh per year figure as derived with that inconsistency stated in its limitations, not as a clean public figure.

Scenario financing screen

Method ID METHOD-TWO-SCENARIO-FINANCE-01

We test each hybrid configuration using indicative debt-service coverage ratio (DSCR), debt-capacity and residual-payment assumptions. Debt is resized in every scenario to a simplified 1.30x minimum DSCR, so identical DSCR values across scenarios are a sizing constraint rather than lender approval. The figures that distinguish the scenarios are the residual or buy-out payment and the owner’s result after buy-out. Both depend on unverified timing, pricing, financing and contract assumptions, so we label them as calculated results with low confidence.

Siting opportunity and constraint screen

Method ID METHOD-SITING-SCREEN-01

Public maps and mine layout are screened in two stages: first the digitised public-source context, then an interpreted opportunity-and-constraint screen. The resulting PV and BESS/PCS areas are preliminary desktop search areas — not equipment footprints, approved buildable land or owner-selected sites — and are always typed owner_confirmation_required.

Development sequence

The F1–F4 screen sits at the front of a sequence that runs from commercial need and site control through approvals, operating design and financing. The map is used to identify which decision must be closed before the next stage can begin.
